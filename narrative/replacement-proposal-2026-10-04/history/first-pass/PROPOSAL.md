# The Mercy of Morning

One replacement story proposal for owner review. Not approved canon and not an implementation brief. The earlier bodily-support story supplies no required premises or characters.

## Premise

Blaise Bloom lives in a city where a benefactor can return everyone to the same beautiful morning. The gift once saved Blaise's life. He has since used it to recover a relationship he is afraid to let develop.

After the latest return, the man he loves is missing. The benefactor says that Julian has chosen to cease existing. Searching for him leads Blaise to discover the limits of a real divine-seeming power, his own part in its apparent omniscience, and a harder problem than whether he has been deceived: if love has intelligible causes, what more has he been demanding that would make it love?

## Blaise and the people he cannot explain away

Blaise is an actor with an excellent memory and a particular pleasure in making somebody laugh when they are trying to remain angry with him. He loves Julian Quince, a maker of plays and painted scenery. He wants a life with him. He also wants their love to certify that he is necessary: that beneath every accident of meeting, disposition, need and circumstance, Julian would still have chosen him.

He can see the difficulty in this demand. A person with no history or desires would have no reason to choose him. Nevertheless, being loved for reasons feels dangerously like being replaceable. The city's patron seems to offer the missing assurance. He brought Blaise back from the edge of death. He can describe a conversation before it happens. Perhaps the circumstances through which Julian loves him express a purpose that cannot fail.

Blaise's privileged memory gradually turns affection into an unequal collaboration. He remembers which joke worked, which apology was accepted and which subject ruined an evening. Julian experiences a lover who understands him with uncanny precision, then a lover who becomes impatient when he departs from a response he has never given. Blaise insists that he is removing accidental obstacles to their happiness. He is also depriving Julian of the experiences through which he might become someone Blaise cannot anticipate.

Julian is neither an innocent emblem of freedom nor a concealed saint. He likes an audience, sometimes more than he likes the person who has helped him find one. He has let Blaise do the tiresome work of making his ambitions possible. He still wants Blaise's company, his timing and his body. He also wants to make a play that can have a second performance, altered by what happened at the first. His wish to leave the city begins as an artistic and social ambition. It becomes an urgent personal need when he understands why their arguments never seem to advance.

Erasmus Rochester, the patron, genuinely provides the city's extraordinary comfort. He remembers the dead he has restored, the reconciliations he has made possible and the people who begged for another morning. He cooks for visitors because he enjoys feeding them, and he can become sulky when a guest prefers the plain bread. His extraordinary power has given him a long, solitary experience of other people. He wants someone who knows the extent of it to call him good without being made to do so. Blaise's survival and gratitude became the beginning of such a relationship. Erasmus's claim to be God grows out of that need, although he no longer regards it as a claim that needs defending.

Vera Harlowe is an older astronomer and an atrocious amateur performer. She wants to establish that the stars are not decorations put up for the city. She also wants Erasmus to acknowledge that she was right. This second desire has kept her within his hospitality long after she had grounds to distrust his account of it. Her delight in winning an argument can make her careless with a person who has just lost the belief that made his life bearable. Julian wants her to design the sky for his theatre outside the city. She keeps postponing her departure until she has given her public demonstration.

Their proposed names are intentional. Julian's given name invokes Julian of Norwich's difficult affirmation of love, while Quince invokes a maker's limited, awkward capacity to give others a world they can enter. Erasmus draws on a humanist's resistance to a simple account of necessity; Rochester gives his hospitality the danger of deciding what a beloved guest is allowed to know. Vera's name joins Rubin's discovery through the motions of actual things to Harlowe's resistance to a prescribed happiness. Neither those names nor Blaise's Pascal and Bloom sources prescribe the characters' personalities.

## The world and the gift

The city is called Aubade. Its white streets climb the inside of a vast curved sky. Looking up from the market, Blaise can see somebody hanging washing upside down on a terrace a mile above him. At night the dark sky settles close enough to the highest roofs for children to push their hands into it. The stars are cold and leave a metallic smell. People argue about whether this makes them suitable for cooling wine.

Outside the city's curved edge lies a large inhabited country. Rain can stand motionless there for years, with settlements built along its dry side. Long stairways connect gardens whose horizons disagree. It is another part of the same extraordinary world, not an ordinary real world waiting to explain away the city. Leaving Aubade does not amount to leaving experience for reality in itself.

The return has a plain, limited action. Erasmus can restore the people and things still within Aubade to their condition on the morning of its dedication. He cannot summon someone or something that has left its reach. He remembers the intervening occurrences and can preserve one invited witness's memory through a return. The city can then develop differently because the witness acts differently and because the country beyond it has continued. A remembered occurrence is a source of formidable foresight. It is not a complete view of every possible future.

These are the story's invented facts. The plot will not require the player to maintain a machine that produces them. Nor will a later revelation expand the power whenever a reversal needs it.

The citizens know that a return is possible. Many have been grateful for one. They do not know how many mornings Erasmus has restored, what happened during all the intervening lives, or how often Blaise has asked him to do it. Leaving is possible. Some residents have left. Most have people, pleasures and hopes in Aubade; the possibility of recovering a terrible loss is a substantial reason to remain. The city becomes a prison through what its repeated restoration prevents particular people from learning and carrying forward, not through a revelation that every resident has secretly wanted to escape.

## The story, including its ending

Blaise and Julian are preparing a comedy for the dedication anniversary. Julian has made an enormous paper horse that will not fit through the door. Blaise knows the horse's left hind leg will fall off during rehearsal. He catches it before it can hit Julian, who is briefly delighted and then annoyed: Blaise has begun laughing at a joke Julian has not yet told.

They have a good afternoon. Blaise performs a pompous magistrate so badly that Julian has to sit down. Their affection is physical, habitual and still wanted. Underneath it, Julian is planning to leave. A small company outside Aubade has offered him a place to build a theatre beside a motionless rainstorm. He wants Blaise to come. He has included a role written for him, but Blaise resents being invited into a future somebody else has already begun to imagine.

The reader learns here, rather than through a late explanatory tragedy, why the gift mattered. A falling bridge once crushed Blaise during a performance. Julian went to Erasmus while Blaise was still alive and asked for the morning again. They agreed that Blaise would remember enough to prevent the accident; Julian did not want to carry the sight of him dying. The return worked. The bridge was repaired. The friends who would have been hurt beneath it were never hurt in that occurrence. That happiness was real.

Subsequent returns did not all answer comparable disasters. Blaise remembers the gradual lowering of his threshold: a cruel thing said in public, a betrayed confidence, an evening in which Julian admitted that remaining here was making him unhappy. Erasmus never described this as deleting a person. He offered Blaise a chance to meet him better. Blaise began to believe that an accidental bad conversation could obstruct a love which, considered properly, was meant to endure.

On the night after the rehearsal, Julian leaves with part of the scenery. Blaise goes to Erasmus instead of following him. He asks for the morning. This is the first return the player witnesses. It is beautiful and frightening: the city's descending dark lifts like a wet cloth, people finish sentences they began days ago, and a broken bowl on the patron's table becomes whole beneath Blaise's hand. The pleasure of being spared an irrevocable act arrives before he has named that act honestly.

Julian does not reappear.

Erasmus says that he felt Julian withdraw from existence. He offers Blaise a religious interpretation of the loss: a creature can refuse the place that love has given him. Blaise cannot accept this, partly because it is monstrous and partly because it implies that Julian has refused him more completely than death could have done. He begins looking for a physical departure. Erasmus lets him search. He believes that outside the city Julian will fail, suffer and come back; he also wants Blaise to discover that their shared refuge is preferable without having to be ordered to believe it.

The search first produces absences with a common shape. The painted door Julian took from the theatre is missing after the return too. So is the cart in which he carried it. The unreturned objects do not prove where he is, but they defeat Erasmus's claim that Julian alone made an inexplicable metaphysical refusal. Vera has a drawing of the city boundary made on a strip of cloth that she left outside it. It retains changes that the corresponding cloth inside has lost. She has been learning how to preserve an investigation across mornings without being the patron's chosen witness. Her astronomical notes also record changes in the outer sky that Erasmus's familiar predictions do not account for.

Blaise follows the cart's actual journey. This part of the story gives him a world with claims on his attention beyond his missing lover: a market held in the dry hollow of a stationary downpour, an inn where the residents arrange their beds to share a sunrise from several directions, performers who like Julian's work but think his promised opening date ridiculous. The people have not been waiting for Blaise to discover that they exist. He can be delighted by them while still wanting to leave as soon as they cease to help his search.

He finds Julian building a stage. Julian is alive. He has had several days in which the morning was not taken back. He has made friends, spoiled some expensive paint and discovered that he is less competent at constructing a roof than he believed. He is glad that Blaise is alive and furious that Blaise has brought the old argument with him. He does not declare their affection retrospectively unreal. He asks why that affection has become a reason that he must remain the person Blaise remembers.

The dispute changes when Blaise learns that Julian left partly because of knowledge Blaise himself gave him. During the rehearsal, Blaise had corrected a line by quoting a version of the play Julian had not yet written in this occurrence. Julian took the discrepancy to Vera. She could not tell him what he would have thought in the lost evenings. She could show him that there had been evenings to lose. His departure was a considered response to evidence, affection and ambition. It was neither an uncaused act nor a proof that he had ceased to love Blaise.

Blaise wants to turn this into a liberating conclusion: Erasmus deceived both of them; therefore everything Blaise did was part of Erasmus's design. Julian refuses that account. The patron offered a possibility. Blaise knew what Julian had said before he asked for the return. Explaining the offer and the desire does not turn the request into an event for which there was nobody to answer.

This is the midpoint, not the solution. Blaise has found Julian and exposed one false account of the disappearance. He has lost the explanation that made his own life seem providential. He has not discovered whether a life entirely within causes can be his, whether the love he preserved was less real than the love which now opposes him, or what a good future could consist in if no one guarantees that it ends well.

He returns to Aubade with Vera's outer-sky observations and Julian's answer. Instead of quietly accepting another private reassurance, he makes his own repeated requests public. He can admit all of them or try to present only those he can still call rescues. That choice changes who will trust his subsequent account; it does not send the story into a different country. Vera agrees to give the public demonstration she has postponed. The theatre becomes the place where residents can hear what has been happening to their lives.

Erasmus answers them. He does not suddenly become powerless or stupid. His explanations of the original rescue are correct. The restored people are real people. The joy of an earlier afternoon was not counterfeit because it occurred before a later disillusionment. He asks Vera why a fact discovered last should possess more authority than a happiness experienced first. He asks Blaise why Julian's wish to leave must be his authentic wish, while Julian's earlier wish to remain must now be dismissed as ignorance. And he asks the residents whether an irreversible death would somehow make their present love more genuine.

Some want another return after hearing everything. That is not presented as a failure of intelligence. Vera herself has spent years wanting a particular acknowledgment from Erasmus. She finds it harder to relinquish that hope when he listens seriously to her work than when he merely dismisses it.

Blaise also confronts the positive alternative to a designing patron. Vera thinks that a cause standing outside reality is a contradiction: if it exists, thinks and acts, its activity belongs to what needs to be understood. The world is not a product waiting for a separate maker to justify it. She argues for one self-existent reality whose particular expressions include their bodies and understanding. A genuinely adequate understanding of a particular love would be an activity of that reality, not an inferior imitation of knowledge possessed by a spectator outside it.

Blaise presses the argument. A demand for explanation does not give them possession of the complete explanation. Their successful knowledge concerns objects under the conditions in which they can experience objects. Vera cannot turn the impossibility of standing outside all experience into a warrant that her thought grasps the world's essence. Nor does placing all desire within one Nature explain why this particular desire should command his assent. Her answer is not that he must give up wanting Julian. A desire can change in its activity when he understands what he desires. At present he wants Julian's love while repeatedly preventing the growth of the understanding through which Julian could love him knowingly.

This disagreement changes the public undertaking. Blaise had wanted Vera to prove that Erasmus was not God, then announce that the city was finally free. He gives up that claim. They can establish the scope of the returns, what was withheld and who asked for them. They cannot turn a discovery about a finite benefactor into an exhaustive explanation of reality. Vera, in turn, stops presenting her star demonstration as if it proved immanence. She has to defend the larger argument separately and accept that Blaise may act with her without accepting it.

Julian comes back before the demonstration. He brings the painted door and several members of the new company. This is not a miraculous response to Blaise's moral progress. He promised Vera a stage from which her work could be seen, and he wants the residents to be able to join the theatre beyond Aubade. He also wants to see Blaise. He does not consider those motives mutually exclusive. He knows that coming inside makes him vulnerable to another return, and he takes the risk because remaining safely outside would leave the whole public account to Blaise and Erasmus.

Blaise initially takes his return as the sign he has wanted: Julian came back despite everything, so some deeper love must have been meant for him. Erasmus can predict much of what Julian will do, although he has not witnessed this whole occurrence before. He knows Julian's pride, loyalty and appetite for an audience. That correct understanding makes Blaise feel robbed of the very sign he was about to claim.

The late central encounter is between the three men at the half-built stage. Julian asks Blaise to help finish a scene they had always postponed because the old ending pleased everybody. He will still leave after the performance. He wants Blaise to play the role. Blaise can grasp why these wishes belong together: Julian wants a continuing collaboration that can survive disappointment, rather than one protected by the removal of its history. That understanding hurts him and also enables a new pleasure. He performs something with Julian that he could not have obtained by reproducing a remembered answer.

Vera gives the more difficult name to this pleasure. If their understanding is adequate, it is not Nature consoling them for the absence of a loving governor. It is Nature understanding a particular activity through their finite minds. The joy belongs to that activity. It does not buy a future reconciliation. Blaise asks why this should be called love of God rather than clear comprehension, and why the truth of an understood relation would preserve anything he means by himself. Vera's claim goes further than comfort: finite understanding participates in the self-understanding of the reality on which it depends. She does not offer surviving memories or an everlasting audience. Blaise can find the claim compelling without pretending that its difficult premises have become settled by the play's success.

The public performance takes place. Its comedy is allowed to work. Vera forgets a line she insisted she did not need to write down. A star drops through the scenery at the wrong moment. Erasmus laughs before anybody else, then tries to hide it. Their pleasure does not erase the account that follows. Blaise has to say what he requested while Julian is present to hear it. Julian can answer a person who remembers doing him harm; he cannot answer an entire city that is about to make the conversation never have happened.

Erasmus offers the morning once more. He does not invent a last-minute catastrophe to make the offer attractive. The existing reasons are enough: people have lost days they would like to recover, they have learned painful truths, and some fear the death which another return could postpone. The patron offers Blaise their original good morning, when Julian wanted to remain, both men were glad to be alive and there was still a promised play ahead of them. He will allow Blaise to forget the intervening history too. He wants Blaise to ask after knowing what the gift does. That freely offered gratitude is the acknowledgment Erasmus has been unable to produce by power alone.

Blaise now sees the patron's demand from within. Erasmus also wants an answer whose causes he can arrange but which will count as unarranged love. This recognition does not make him a joke. Blaise knows what it is like to want it.

The climax resolves the return's use and the personal request, not the whole philosophical dispute. Erasmus has repeatedly promised Blaise that this next return will happen only if Blaise asks for it. The story establishes that promise well before the climax and shows the patron honoring smaller refusals that cost him something. He could break it. Doing so would destroy the informed witness whose acknowledgment he wants. The final conflict is between actual people with power and desires, not a password that disables a supernatural mechanism.

### An unrepeated morning

Blaise refuses the offered return. He does not claim that earlier Julian was false, that pain makes a life authentic, or that irreversible time is inherently virtuous. He refuses to make the presently answering man pay for an earlier happiness by losing the understanding that lets him answer now.

Julian leaves after the performance. Whether he welcomes Blaise into the company soon, or asks for a separation, depends on whether Blaise has told him the truth and respected the decisions he made during their shared story. A declaration at the climax does not repair every earlier act. Their affection can remain while their life together changes or ends.

Blaise initially stays in Aubade. This is not a penance assigned by Julian. He has accepted a part in a new comedy which nobody knows how to finish, and he wants to perform it. He also wants to remain with Erasmus through a morning the patron cannot recite in advance. Erasmus is allowed the indignity and possibility of being a finite friend. Blaise can dispute Vera's account of God and still refuse to treat this friend as either the ground of reality or worthless once he fails to be that ground.

The last scene gives Blaise an audience, an untested line and a letter from Julian that he has not opened. He finishes the scene before reading the letter. He wants to know what it says. His desire has not been removed, and its satisfaction is no longer the sole condition under which the present act can be good.

### The good morning

Blaise asks. Erasmus returns the city, Julian included, and allows Blaise to lose his privileged memory. The morning is genuinely happy. Julian wants him. Their recovered affection is not exposed as a cheap counterfeit by sinister music or a secretly malicious new lover. They enjoy the scene whose future development has just been prevented.

The player has heard Julian's present answer and seen what has been lost. Blaise no longer possesses that knowledge. The final image is the paper horse coming apart and both men laughing as they try to carry it through the door. Erasmus watches them from a place where neither thinks to look. He has obtained the request he wanted, but once again there is nobody beside him who remembers what it meant.

## What makes the philosophy consequential

The patron's finite power is an empirical discovery. It defeats his particular claim to omniscience; it does not prove that reality has no necessary ground, that all loving acts are unfree, or that a world experienced under human conditions is an illusion. Blaise's search for a complete ground is precisely what made a powerful, attentive benefactor seem capable of answering more than any finite cause could answer.

Kant's strongest pressure is double. Objective experience is possible through conditions that do not themselves give knowledge of things as they are independently of those conditions. At the same time, a complete empirical causal account does not by itself settle the problem of freedom and responsibility. Blaise cannot refute freedom by supplying another motive, prove it by surprising Erasmus, or excuse his conduct merely because it belongs to an intelligible history. The story does not make an empirical return a passage into a noumenal realm.

Spinoza offers more than the removal of a personal god. One immanent reality, expressed through attributes and finite modes, would make adequate human understanding a real activity of Nature. Activity and freedom differ from an uncaused choice: understanding can make a person more an adequate cause of what he does. The same appetite can become active or passive. That possibility changes Blaise's aim from obtaining the certified response to understanding and participating in a particular relationship without requiring it to stand outside causes. Intellectual love is the strongest, contested interpretation of the joy in that understanding. It does not guarantee Julian's pardon or Blaise's survival as a store of memories.

The rival arguments remain alive after the concrete conflict is resolved. Vera must answer how her reasoning reaches substance and particular essence; Blaise must answer why demanding a position outside all causes would make love more intelligible rather than impossible. The story makes those arguments tempting through the lives they would let him lead.

## Honest remaining risks in this proposal

The obvious danger is reducing a difficult conflict to the correction of a controlling lover. The shared rescue, present affection, patron's real goods and the earlier-love objection are therefore structural parts of the story, not mitigating biographies to be attached later. They must survive the sample and the full manuscript.

The climax depends on Erasmus's particular desire for an informed acknowledgment and his established promise. If he becomes merely a villain who arbitrarily agrees to lose, it fails. His willingness to honor unwelcome refusals has to be experienced before the final one.

The country and theatre must remain inhabited places with their own pleasures and limits. They cannot simply represent freedom, while Aubade represents error. Likewise, the substantial philosophical scenes must change a proposed act or a desired explanation. They cannot be appended to a complete moral plot as discussions of its themes.

This proposal commits to one connected story and two earned ending directions. Its value should be judged through that story and the accompanying performed scene, not through a projected duration or a promise of future systems.
