# The Mercy of Morning

I recommend a story about a man who asks a god to guarantee that his lover's love is free of causes.

Blaise Bloom lives in a city whose patron can restore its inhabitants to the same beautiful morning. The gift once saved his life. He has since used it to preserve a relationship he is afraid to let develop. After the latest return, the man he loves is missing. Finding him will expose the limits of a real supernatural power and force Blaise to confront the explanations through which he has made his own conduct bearable.

Choices affect disclosure, trust and endings within one shared story. The returns cause new events rather than requiring repeated playthroughs.

Aubade's white streets climb the inside of its curved sky. Above the market, somebody hangs washing upside down a mile overhead. At night children bring down stars, which smell of metal. Beyond the city, settlements grow along the dry sides of motionless rainstorms. The outside belongs to the same strange world.

Erasmus Rochester, Aubade's patron, can restore only people and things still within the city. He cannot bring back someone who has left. He remembers the intervening occurrences and can preserve one invited witness's memory. Blaise has usually been that witness. This gives him a dangerous advantage: he knows the apology Julian accepted, the joke that made him laugh, the subject that spoiled their evening. Julian meets an uncannily attentive lover who becomes impatient when he fails to give an answer he has never given before.

Blaise is an actor. He wants Julian Quince's company, desire and laughter, but also wants their love to prove that he is necessary. Being loved for reasons feels like being replaceable. Yet without a history and desires, Julian would have no reason to choose anyone. Blaise keeps looking for a love no history could change. Erasmus's foresight seems to promise it.

Julian makes plays and scenery. Ambitious, affectionate and vain about his work, he wants to build a theatre outside Aubade. He wants Blaise to come and has promised him a role without asking. Their pleasure and their capacity to misuse one another precede any revelation.

Erasmus has restored people from terrible accidents. He remembers their relief and wants an informed person to call him good without being forced. Vera Harlowe, an older astronomer and dreadful amateur actor, wants to prove that the stars exist beyond the patron's decorations. Her hunger for his acknowledgment has helped keep her inside his hospitality.

The opening follows Blaise and Julian repairing a paper horse and rehearsing a comedy. Their enjoyment is genuine. So was the first rescue: Julian asked Erasmus to restore the morning while Blaise lay dying beneath a fallen bridge. Blaise kept the memory needed to prevent the accident; Julian chose to learn what had happened afterward without retaining the sight. They were glad to be alive together. Later, Blaise's threshold for another return fell from catastrophe to humiliation, betrayal and Julian's wish to leave.

We also see Erasmus honor an unwelcome refusal. He assembles guests to hear Blaise declare that his survival proves he was chosen for a purpose. Blaise refuses. Erasmus dismisses the guests and takes down the commemorative cloth himself. He renews his promise that, while Blaise remains inside Aubade, he will not return it without Blaise's request. This is a costly personal promise, not a magical contract discovered at the climax.

During rehearsal, Blaise quotes a revision Julian has not yet written. Julian takes the discrepancy to Vera. She has been keeping observations beyond the city's edge, where returns do not erase them. Her familiar instrument box lets her recover those records even when she no longer remembers making them. She shows Julian that there have been evenings he was not allowed to carry forward.

Julian leaves with a painted door and a cart. Blaise asks Erasmus for the morning instead of following him. The dark lifts from the city; a broken bowl becomes whole under his hand. Julian does not reappear.

Erasmus says that Julian withdrew from existence. Blaise searches for a physical departure. The cart and door are also missing, although both existed on the morning to which the city returned. Following them takes him through the inhabited country to Julian's unfinished theatre. Julian is alive, has spoiled some expensive paint and is less good at constructing a roof than he supposed.

He still loves Blaise. He refuses to let that fact decide whether he may become somebody Blaise cannot anticipate. His departure followed evidence, ambition and affection. It was caused. That does not make it a counterfeit answer.

Blaise first tries to blame Erasmus for the whole history. Julian insists that the patron's offer does not account away Blaise's request. Blaise knew what Julian had said before asking to remove the evening in which he said it.

Then Blaise does something consequential and frightening: he requests another return, with Vera as the remembered witness. Julian is safely outside its reach. Blaise says he wants to meet him without a store of successful approaches. He also wants to stop knowing Julian's refusal and feel innocent again. Vera objects to the supposed test: different knowledge and circumstances cannot demonstrate an uncaused choice. She accepts the witness's place to preserve testimony and pursue her own interrupted inquiry. Blaise gives her an account of his request to keep outside.

The next scene begins with restored Blaise reading his own account in the comic judge's voice. He finds this former man's explanations absurd. Then he reaches the description of the request which made his present innocence possible. Other people's continuing activities and the changed theatre outside establish that this morning follows that request, however his inner memory orders it. His inability to recall the act does not make the exterior history unreal. Nor does saying “I” disclose an untouched soul behind it.

His next act is new: he interrupts Erasmus's public meal and reads the account in his ordinary voice. If he concealed requests in the document, Vera contradicts him. People whose lives were used in his private explanations begin asking what they have lost. The gathering grows into a public performance and account at the theatre.

Erasmus retains a serious answer. Earlier love was real. An earlier happiness does not become worthless because a later person rejects it. Why should the latest desire always possess authority? Would irreversible death make anybody's love more genuine? Some residents want the gift after learning what it does.

The dispute over God now goes beyond exposing Erasmus. Vera defends a self-existent reality, not merely the idea that everything is connected. Things distinguished only by their changing states cannot thereby establish distinct independent substances. An absolutely infinite substance would leave no attribute through which another could differ. Blaise challenges the crucial step: a conception that requires necessary existence does not yet establish that its object exists. Reason's demand for a complete ground is not possession of that ground.

Vera argues that thought belongs to the reality it understands; adequate knowledge need not be checked by a spectator standing outside existence. Blaise presses the Kantian objection that the conditions making objective experience possible do not grant knowledge of things independently of those conditions. Their disagreement changes the public undertaking. A demonstration of the patron's finite reach cannot prove the whole of Nature, and exposing a lie cannot settle freedom.

Kant also bears on Blaise's conduct. He wrote to his restored self because he thought a reason could make a claim on someone who had not yet felt its force. He cannot dismiss that claim merely by discovering why he wants to evade it. Causal explanation and accountability address the same act differently. No unpredictable refusal becomes a proof of intelligible freedom.

Julian returns for the performance with the painted door and members of his new company. He promised Vera a stage and wants residents to join their theatre outside. He also wants to see Blaise. Erasmus correctly anticipates much of this through understanding Julian. Blaise must face a loving act whose intelligible causes do not give him possession of it.

Julian asks him to finish a scene they always postponed because their old ending pleased everybody. Blaise begins to understand the collaboration Julian wants: one which can develop through disappointment. They achieve a new pleasure together. Vera's stronger claim remains contested. Understanding a particular existence through God's attributes would be Nature's own finite activity; its intellectual love would express God's self-love, without a cosmic person promising affection back. Blaise asks what warrants calling their understanding adequate, and why a truth which does not grow old should preserve the knower he means by himself. A successful performance answers neither objection. Its pleasure remains worth having without Julian's forgiveness as payment.

After the performance, Erasmus offers the morning once more. No new catastrophe makes the choice artificially difficult. The existing goods and losses are enough. He offers the happy Julian who wanted to remain, and offers Blaise forgetfulness too. He wants the request after Blaise knows what it means. Blaise recognizes his own impossible demand in the patron: arranging the causes of an answer while wanting it to count as unarranged love.

In the forward ending, Blaise refuses. He does not call the earlier Julian false or suffering necessary. He will not recover that happiness by taking away the understanding from which the present Julian answers. Erasmus honors his promise. This gives people an unrepeated interval to leave or consider the gift; it does not abolish recurrence throughout the universe.

Julian leaves. Whether he soon welcomes Blaise into the company or asks for a separation grows from Blaise's actual conduct, not a final declaration. Blaise initially stays. He wants to perform in a new comedy nobody knows how to finish, and to accompany Erasmus through a morning the patron cannot recite. The last scene gives him an audience, an untested line and an unopened letter from Julian. He finishes before reading it. He still desperately wants to know what it says.

In the other ending, Blaise asks. Both men return to their genuinely happy morning and Blaise loses his privileged memory. They laugh together as the paper horse comes apart. The player knows what this joy has cost. Erasmus watches from a place where neither thinks to look, once again without anyone beside him who remembers what the request meant.

The main risk is making this only a correction of a controlling lover. The mutual rescue, earlier-love objection, self-initiated loss of memory and patron's real generosity are essential to preventing that simplification. Hello Charlotte's useful lesson here is how familiar pleasure changes meaning as attachment deepens, rather than how to conceal a missing relationship beneath a late cosmological reveal. The accompanying scene is the first test of whether this story earns that distinction.

The proposed names have verified historical-given and literary-surname sources: Blaise Pascal and Leopold Bloom; Julian of Norwich and Peter Quince; Desiderius Erasmus and Edward Rochester; Vera Rubin and Clarissa Harlowe. Their symbolic tensions inform these particular characters without importing the originals' personalities.
