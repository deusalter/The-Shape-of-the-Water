# The horse's other end

A scene from the proposed opening, before the first witnessed return. Close third person follows Blaise. This is proposed story material, not installed content.

Blaise caught the horse's leg before it fell.

Julian remained inside the horse, swearing. From the wardrobe came the scrape of paper against paper, then a soft tearing sound.

“Don't pull,” Blaise said.

“I am trying to become smaller.”

The theatre had been a dining room. Its long window looked across the square at a street that climbed steadily until the houses hung above their own roofs. Someone on the upper terrace was beating a rug. Dust rose through the window instead of settling, and Julian had spent the morning trying to keep it out of the wet paint.

Blaise set the leg against the wall. He held the wardrobe door open and took the horse by its enormous chin. Julian crawled backward, bringing out a pair of human feet first, then the other three legs, then a mane made from the fringe of Erasmus's discarded dressing gown.

“I measured it,” Julian said.

“The wardrobe?”

“The head.”

Blaise laughed.

Julian's shoulders stopped moving.

“That wasn't the funny part.”

“It was quite good.”

“No. I was going to say I forgot about the horse's other end.”

“That is better.”

Julian waited. Blaise could have laughed again. He had done so once before, a short, embarrassed sound which made the rest of the afternoon unpleasant. Instead he bent to retrieve the fallen leg.

It had come off in his hand this time. Previously it had struck Julian's ankle. Anticipating something was not always an offence. He had prevented an injury, although Julian would laugh at the grandeur of that description. An ankle bumped by paper. A little less pain in the world. Surely he was allowed that.

Julian took the leg from him.

“Thank you.”

He said it without irony. The relief was so immediate that Blaise wanted to thank him for it.

They mended the horse at the long table. Julian held the joint while Blaise wound string around it. Beneath the paper, the wooden ribs were visible. Julian had written figures on one of them, crossed them out, then written the same figures larger.

“I see you checked your measurements.”

“I checked my confidence.”

This time Blaise laughed at the right moment.

Julian leaned against him to reach the scissors. Blaise let his hand rest on the back of Julian's neck. A little dried paint came away beneath his thumb. He liked Julian like this, occupied and warm, impatient with an object which had refused to become exactly what he imagined. Julian turned and kissed him, then took the scissors from behind his elbow.

Nothing had been extracted from him. Blaise had not supplied the required phrase. He had been standing there, holding a piece of string, and Julian had wanted to kiss him.

But he had stood where Julian would reach across him. Had he known that? His memory contained so many small arrangements that he could no longer reliably separate an anticipation from a wish. Perhaps the kiss had followed because Julian was grateful for the repaired horse, because Blaise had laughed, because he was accustomed to finding him agreeable at this hour.

Why did discovering a cause always feel like discovering a substitute?

If Julian had kissed him for no reason, the act would have belonged to nobody. A twitch could have done as much. Blaise wanted the kiss to follow from Julian's affection, from the particular man who had been here yesterday. Yet once he had given the affection a history, he found himself looking behind it for something that the history had not produced.

He was easy to love because he was useful, perhaps. Or because he was funny. A funnier, more useful man could then stand in his place. The conditions would be satisfied; the world would proceed without noticing the exchange.

Julian was turning the scissors in his hand.

“You're pulling,” he said.

Blaise released the string.

“Sorry.”

“I like the leg attached. It makes the marriage possible.”

The horse was to marry a judge in the last scene. The play had once concerned a dispute over an orchard. Julian had become bored with the orchard.

He fetched Blaise's robe from the back of a chair. It was a curtain with sleeves.

“Just the entrance. I need to know how long it takes you to get around the table.”

“Dignity cannot be hurried.”

“Dignity can go around the other side.”

Blaise put on the robe. It trailed behind him for several feet. He stood very still, then lifted a hand as though granting the furniture permission to exist.

Julian's breathing changed. Blaise heard the laugh he was trying to prevent and prolonged the gesture.

“Your Honour,” Julian said, and failed to go on.

Blaise bowed to the horse.

“I have considered your proposal. My advisers are concerned about the disparity in our stations.”

“He's a judge too,” Julian managed.

“Then I withdraw my objection to his eating them.”

Julian sat on the table. He was laughing properly now, one hand pressed flat against the wood. Blaise went around the room with such delicate solemnity that the hem caught beneath his heel. He recovered, pretended that the stumble was a bow and heard Julian laugh harder.

He wanted to do the whole scene. He wanted Julian to remain there until evening, unable to ask him for anything except another line.

“Keep that,” Julian said. “The bow.”

“And the advisers?”

“The advisers can go. I like you falling over.”

Blaise made an injured sound and gathered up the robe's long hem.

Julian had stopped laughing. He was looking at a folded letter beside the paint pot.

“They've made the roof high enough,” he said.

“Who has?”

“The company I told you about. Beyond the west road.”

“You told me they hadn't decided where to put the stage.”

“They hadn't then.”

There was a drawing inside the letter. A black wall of rain rose behind a narrow platform. Somebody had added a horse much too large for it.

“They've seen your measurements,” Blaise said.

“I drew the horse.”

Julian smoothed the paper against the table.

“I told them you might do the judge.”

“Might?”

“I told them you'd do it. I shouldn't have. I've written back.”

“What did you tell them this time?”

“That I would ask you.”

Blaise waited for the question. Julian did not ask it.

Outside, the rug-beater had stopped. A loose thread drifted upward past the window, curling toward the dark underside of the street.

“I thought you were happy here,” Blaise said.

“I am, sometimes.”

“You said you were.”

“Yes.”

There was no embarrassment in the answer. Julian seemed to think he could admit having been happy without conceding anything about what he owed to that happiness.

“I don't understand what has changed.”

“I want to see what happens to the play after somebody sees it.”

“People can see it here.”

“And afterward?”

Blaise looked at the letter. The drawn rain had been made with hundreds of little strokes. Someone had spent an afternoon on a place neither of them had visited together.

“Erasmus doesn't return the city every night,” he said.

“How often does he?”

“When somebody needs him to.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

Julian put the letter away. He did not slam a drawer or turn his back. He remained beside Blaise, close enough that the edge of his sleeve touched his hand.

“I asked him once,” Julian said. “I know what it can mean to need it.”

Blaise remembered the bridge coming down. Less the impact than the small white piece of sky visible between Julian's fingers. Julian had been holding something against his chest. He had kept saying Blaise's name as though the repetition itself could prevent the thing it named from disappearing.

They had wanted the morning. Both of them. Afterward they had sat on the unbroken bridge, eating bread, and Julian had become angry because Blaise said they ought to go and do something with the day. For an hour, Julian wanted to do nothing at all.

Was that wish worth less because it came before this one?

If the later Julian could overrule the earlier, then tomorrow's wish could overrule this departure too. Why should Blaise cooperate in destroying a happiness merely because its opponent was speaking last? There was no virtue in lateness. A cruel impulse could arrive after a generous one. Discovery could improve a judgment; fatigue could spoil it. Time did not adjudicate between them.

Erasmus remembered both. Perhaps that was what Blaise needed from him: a judgment which did not mistake the order of desires for their worth.

Then he noticed where his thought had taken him. He wanted Erasmus to decide which Julian was entitled to answer. Naturally he hoped the entitled one would love him best.

The hope did not make the argument false. That was the refuge he always reached. A hungry man could correctly identify bread. Wanting a thing did not refute the reasons for wanting it.

“I want you to come,” Julian said.

Blaise had been preparing to oppose a departure in which he had no place. The invitation made him feel strangely cheated.

“And if I don't?”

Julian rubbed a little dried glue between his fingers. It came away in a translucent curl.

“I think I'll go.”

He could ask what Julian had meant by bringing him back if he was only going to leave. Once, that question had brought Julian across the room. Blaise remembered the relief of feeling him kneel against his legs, and how carefully he had avoided mentioning the question afterward.

He said, “Help me out of this thing.”

The curtain had twisted around his waist. Julian untied it. For a moment his arms encircled Blaise from behind. Blaise shut his eyes and allowed himself the contact without saying anything that would make Julian account for it.

“We haven't tried the final speech,” Julian said.

“You haven't finished it.”

“I have something.”

He fetched the pages and gave Blaise the top one.

The judge had lost his office, his house and the horse. At the bottom of the page he announced that he would remain where he was until the roses apologized.

Blaise remembered Julian reading it to him in bed. The line had not worked. Together they had changed it.

“Until the roses admit they were weeds,” Blaise said.

Julian became very still.

The silence was new. Blaise had no account of how long it would last.

“What?” Julian said.

“It's funnier.”

“I know it's funnier.”

“You told me there hadn't been another return while we were writing this.”

Julian took the page. On the back was a single word, written small enough to hide beneath his thumb.

Blaise could not see it. He knew what it was.

“Did you look?”

“At what?”

Julian turned the paper over. Weeds.

It would have been easy to say yes. A little intrusion, immediately forgivable. Blaise could confess to looking through Julian's pages and receive the anger due to a much smaller act.

“No,” he said.

Julian waited.

Blaise wanted to explain the evening in bed, the warm place at his back, how much he had loved helping to make the line. To Julian it would be a thing Blaise had taken from a future he had not been permitted to reach. To Blaise it was something they had done together. He could not make himself call it theft without also seeming to deny that it had been theirs.

Julian folded the page once.

“I'm taking the door to Vera.”

“Why?”

“She said she'd help me paint the sky on the other side.”

“Now?”

“While it's light.”

He went to the painted door at the rear of the stage and lifted it from its loose hinges. The horse was in his way. Blaise moved it aside.

Julian paused with the door against his shoulder.

“You could tell me,” he said.

Blaise looked down at the robe on the floor. He could still hear the afternoon in which Julian had laughed at the revised line. It remained a fact. No amount of fear could make it not have happened.

“It was ours,” he said.

Julian adjusted his grip and carried the door outside.
