# L3 ending reading: second-crossing

Bounded author reading, not a complete player route. Required preceding encounters are recorded in ENDING-L3-AUDITIONS.md. The prose below selects one branch at each decision and contains no unseen sibling variants.

The two Miriams sit at opposite ends of the workshop table. The one nearest the sink has her coat on the back of the chair. The one near the door has put the bag of peas on a plate. Meltwater has reached the table through the split in the bag.

Ada brings a second plate. She sets it beneath the first.

“That one's cracked,” she says.

Both women look at the plate. You had been waiting for them to do something together, and the small agreement makes you feel worse than the differences did.

Simon has put the marked strip in the middle of the table. Beside it lies the unmarked piece. They are the same size. A splinter rises beside the split in each. When he presses one down, the other remains raised.

“Leave them,” Ada says. “We can see.”

He moves his hands away.

Miriam at the sink asks you to sit down. You have been standing against the closed cupboard because you can see both faces from there.

“Which one?” you ask.

“Any chair.”

You sit sideways, one knee beneath the table and one outside it. The cupboard handle stops pressing against your back. You feel its shape for a few seconds after you have moved.

“I want to ask you something about Ada,” you say.

Ada makes a sound in her throat.

“About what happened before I came back.”

“Ask it,” she says.

You turn toward the coat on the chair.

“When she put her hand here.” You hold your wrist. “You saw that.”

“Yes.”

“And what she said?”

“I didn't hear it clearly. I was by the entrance.”

You had wanted the three words. You try not to supply them.

“What did you see?”

“The chair caught you as you stood. She steadied it. She touched your wrist. You went toward Simon.”

“She was there.”

“Yes.”

Ada looks down at the wrist you are still holding.

“I don't mind being asked,” she says. “I mind being told I remember.”

“I'm not telling you.”

“All right.”

You let go of your wrist. There are four pale marks where your fingers were. They redden while you watch.

The Miriam by the door moves the peas away from her hand. The skin beneath the cloth is damp. She flexes one finger, then stops.

“Have you asked what happened to you?” she says.

“I was carrying the glass.”

“I heard that part.”

“I came back.”

“You're certain that was you.”

“I remember it.”

“I remember going into the cabinet. So does she.”

The woman at the sink does not look away from you. You had thought of her memory as the one that might help. For a moment you resent her for keeping the rest of it.

“I don't have somebody else here saying he remembers being me.”

“Would his arriving make your memory stop being yours?” she asks.

You look at the cupboard. Its door is closed. No one has suggested that somebody is behind it. Your eyes keep giving it that possibility.

“Don't,” Ada says.

The Miriam at the sink puts her hands flat on the table. “I'm not trying to summon him.”

“I know,” you say.

You do not know where to put your eyes now. Simon offers you his chair, which faces the door. You shake your head.

“I'd want something he couldn't have,” you say. “Something I hadn't put into words. Then he'd tell me that too, and I'd have to go farther in.”

Simon rubs his face. “There isn't a last cupboard.”

You look at the cupboard again.

“Sorry,” he says. “I meant the test. You would keep choosing another memory.”

“Would you believe him?”

“I don't know what believing him would settle. If he knew something you knew, we'd have to account for that.”

“I'm asking which one you'd speak to.”

He looks at the two women.

“The one who asked me the question. Then the other one.”

“I tried to work out which of us ought to be saying I,” the Miriam by the door says.

The other woman closes her eyes.

“I couldn't stop thinking it, even while I tried to give it to you.”

“Neither could I,” says the woman at the sink.

Their voices resemble each other most when they are quiet. From the concourse, someone who could not see the table might think one woman had paused and then answered herself. You can see the second breath begin before the first mouth has closed.

Simon asks whether they would prefer him to leave. Both say no. This time you can tell which no was first.

“Is there anything about a person that you think death can't take?” you ask.

The woman by the door turns the plate a little. She looks across the table before answering.

“I think there is something eternal about understanding. But I don't mean that this whole life is kept running, somewhere out of sight.”

“She knows a day you don't.”

“I know.”

“Could that day make her understand something you can't?”

“Of course.”

“Then if it can be added, it can be lost.”

“This person can lose a capacity. I don't think our minds are exempt from that.”

You look from her wrapped hand to the dry dressing on the other hand. You can understand why one should hurt more. The resemblance keeps arriving faster than the reason.

The woman at the sink speaks.

“I don't think the value of understanding lies in having a recording that runs forever. When I understand why something follows, I am doing something I couldn't do by keeping every picture of it. I mean that activity when I speak of a mind taking part in the understanding of Nature.”

“You're saying a truth doesn't stop being true when the person dies. How does that keep any of the person who understood it?”

“I'm saying more than that. A mind's understanding belongs to the nature it knows. Insofar as it understands how its own existence follows from that nature, it's a particular way that nature understands itself. I think that has an eternity which doesn't consist in carrying this afternoon into another afternoon.”

“Both of you?”

“That claim could apply to both of us. It doesn't tell us why there are two people at this table. I haven't arrived at that understanding by looking at these pieces of wood.”

“And it doesn't tell me which of you I could lose.”

“No.”

“And she could do that without remembering what you remember.”

“Some of it. Other things would have to be learned.”

“Would you give the day away, then?”

She pulls her coat toward her knees.

“No.”

“I thought...”

“No, Blaise. I don't have to want to lose it.”

The Miriam by the door looks down. You wait for her to improve the answer. She presses a dry corner of the cloth against her finger and leaves it alone.

She asks whether you would like the window open. You say yes. Ada goes to open it, but Miriam gets up first.

The window sticks. She pulls the lower edge toward her and lifts it again. Outside, a bicycle chain runs through its gears. She watches the bicycle go, then comes back to the table.

You have no memory against which to compare the sound.

Miriam by the door asks the other woman whether she can look at the dressing. She lifts her hand. They lean toward each other across the table.

The lower edge is coming unstuck. The younger wound is on the hand that reaches to smooth it.

“That will stick to you,” Ada says.

Both women stop. The one with the coat laughs once, without looking up. Ada tears off a clean strip and gives it to them.

Simon asks you to come to the service passage. You know which handle is there. You cannot picture the door without also picturing your hand on the entrance.

He does not ask twice.

The service door stands open. Ada has put a block under it. The yard contains the two bins. The bag is still wedged beneath the lid of one. From here you can see the place where the cardboard caught.

Simon stays on the room side of the threshold. He points to a pencil mark on the floor, then lowers his hand when you look at it.

“We shouldn't call that the boundary,” he says. “It's where I can put the camera without its foot being in the way.”

You sit on the sound chair. Its seat is cold through your trousers. The faulty one remains in the workshop, turned toward the wall.

“I want to know what would happen if I did it again.”

“So do I.”

“And if I don't?”

He takes a moment. “Then we have the event we've already got.”

“You won't keep asking.”

“No.”

Ada has followed you as far as the washbasin. She is unrolling the tape Miriam gave back to her, searching for its edge.

You ask whether going through without the parcel would tell them anything. Simon says it would test one difference. You ask whether he thinks the glass did it. He says there is no result that establishes that, and he would rather you did not carry a broken mirror for the sake of removing the doubt.

You picture yourself arriving empty-handed even though you already were. You picture Ada on the bucket. You have begun to miss things while they are still in front of you.

“I could go sooner,” you say.

“Than what?” Ada asks.

“Than last time.”

“You'd still be going now.”

You almost laugh. She finds the tape's edge and lays the roll on the basin.

Simon says that another return, if it happened, would give a second occasion for comparison. The result would be stronger about the repeated sequence and weaker than you might want about its cause. It would not establish that all the same causes had occurred again. He points at you, then realizes he has done what Ada asked you not to do.

“Your remembering,” he says. “That would already be different.”

“I know.”

“Yes.”

You ask the question you have been leaving behind the others.

“Could I get to her?”

Ada stops unrolling the tape. You wish you had asked while she was farther away.

“The one at the chair,” you say.

Simon looks into the yard. “Nothing we've seen shows that this would take you there.”

“It doesn't show it couldn't.”

“No.”

“You think that's a bad reason.”

He sits on the floor beside the camera. “I think it's the reason you have.”

Ada says she is going back to the table. You ask her to wait.

“For what?”

You can hear the mouth answering before you speak. This time it is your own voice.

You tell her that you know the two chairs might happen again. You know you could put the pan between them, or leave it where it was, or ask the question about the singer. None of that is what you are asking for.

“Then what are you asking for?” she says.

You say you want the person who was there before you knew you could lose her.

Ada takes the sound chair. You stand beside the washbasin.

“You knew me before this weekend.”

“Yes.”

“I wasn't gone every time I went out of the room.”

“I didn't think you were.”

“And now?”

The door opens on the yard. A fly passes through it, turns beside the basin and settles on the tape.

“Now I don't know what happens when I stop being there.”

“I can tell you some of it. Most of it would bore you.”

“It wouldn't.”

“It would.”

She watches the fly. You can see that she is deciding whether to lift the tape. She leaves it.

“I haven't offered to be her for you,” she says.

“I know.”

“I could come out through the front with you.”

“Do you want to?”

“I want to get outside. I'd like you to come.”

You look at the service door. Ada waits on the chair. She does not place a hand where you can accidentally touch it.

You ask them to come to the service passage before deciding where to stand. Simon carries the camera. The Miriam who waited outside brings her coat. The other leaves the peas on the plate.

You say what you mean to do. You will cross outward without carrying the glass. You do not know whether it will happen again. If it does, the people in the room may no longer remember the conversation you are having now. The woman who waited outside may still be able to tell you that it happened. You cannot promise that the next occasion will leave her unaffected merely because this one did.

“Then I won't stand in the yard,” she says.

She means the service yard. She will wait by the front entrance this time, on this side of the road. The distinction has become important without anyone proving why it should be.

Ada asks whether you intend to bring her to the chair again. You say you will not arrange the chair behind your legs.

“You can still ask me things,” she says.

“You might not know why.”

“Then tell me.”

Simon says he will stay beside the camera on the room side. The Miriam without the coat says she will stay where she can see him. She asks you to say when you are going, then changes her mind.

“Say when you've decided to go. Don't count us down.”

You go with the woman carrying her coat as far as the front entrance. She puts the coat on, passes you and steps onto the pavement. You remain on the tiles, holding the door. She moves to the left of the doorway, clear of the opening, and rests her good hand against the wall.

“Here,” she says.

You tell her you have seen where she is. You let the entrance close and go back through the bath. She has not accompanied you back to the service passage.

Simon asks whether he may start the camera. He shows you the frame: your lower legs, the threshold and the bottom of the outside wall. You say yes. He asks Ada and Miriam to stay behind it if they do not want to be included. Both move beside him. He starts recording and tells you it has started. You have not yet decided to cross.

You say you have decided. Your voice comes back from under the gallery after you have stopped speaking.

The threshold is worn lower in the middle. You choose the middle. You place one foot beyond it and feel the roughness of the yard through your sole. The other foot lifts.

Your right hand is on painted metal.

The largest piece of mirror rests against the rabbit float. You can see your forehead between the ears. Ada sits on the bucket with the saucepan. Simon is turning his phone face down.

You know the sentence. You let him say it.

“The sight worked. Whatever else happened. I need that understood.”

There is the lift on sight. It arrives with the moving mouth. You have not spoken the first word over it.

Miriam is on the bench. One sock is wet. She notices you looking at the hand wrapped around the peas and shifts the bag toward her knee.

“What is it?” she asks.

You look at Ada's wrist, at the saucepan, at the room side of the service door. None of them tells you whether the woman who went outside is still there.

You open the front entrance.

The Miriam in the coat is standing to the left of the doorway. She has one hand against the wall. She turns toward you, then looks past you into the bath.

“Did you go?” she asks.

“Yes.”

“I was trying to hear.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

You show her your empty hands. She looks at them as if there should be something to take.

Inside, the other Miriam has begun to stand. Ada rises from the bucket and reaches toward her good elbow. The Miriam in the coat sees the movement and makes room in the doorway, although no one is coming through it yet.

“Who is that?” Simon says.

You ask the woman outside whether she remembers the last conversation.

She tells you what you asked her to do. She tells you where she said she would wait. She remembers your saying that she might not be safe just because she had remained outside once before.

You ask her whether Ada offered to come out through the front with you.

“I wasn't with you,” Miriam answers. “I heard you call us to the passage.”

You had not told her what Ada offered. You look past her, into the room. Ada is still helping the other woman stand.

Simon repeats his question. The Miriam in the coat asks whether she may come in. He moves away from the entrance, looking at the bench as he does it.

You step aside. She passes you. For a moment the two women are facing one another with nobody between them.

The one by the bench puts the peas down. The other keeps her coat on.

“Wait,” the first says.

The other woman stops.

You are still holding the entrance open. The closer pulls against your hand. Ada asks you to shut it, and you say you will in a moment.
