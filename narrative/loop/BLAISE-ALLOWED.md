## Passage

Simon has put a second piece of paper beneath your prediction so it will not pick up moisture from the table. You had expected him to keep finding reasons to put it aside. Now you want him to stop looking at it.

The current result is written beneath the prediction, with the time and the people who saw it. It is a very small amount of paper for the way your stomach feels.

“I keep thinking I've been allowed,” you say.

Simon looks up. “Allowed what?”

“Another try.”

“By whom?”

You had hoped that could remain inside the word. You rub the spot on your thumb where the handle pressed it. It is no longer sore.

“God, if you want me to say it.”

Miriam says she did not know you thought about God. You tell her you did not know either, in this particular way. You have thought about punishment, and being grateful, and whether either requires someone to be listening. You have not usually said these things while someone else was waiting to get past you with a bucket.

Ada waits with the bucket. You move your chair. She passes behind it and says she will be in the workshop when there is something she can test.

“There was someone who knew what I meant,” you tell Miriam. “Now she doesn’t remember being that person. I’m still here, remembering her. I want to know whether I’ve been allowed to find her again.”

“Your remembering would have to be part of the account,” she answers. “So would her not remembering.”

“That's not what I mean.”

“I know.”

“Do you? If someone opened a door so I could reach a person I thought I’d lost, you wouldn’t leave the intention out of the explanation. You would ask who opened it.”

Simon folds his hands around the edge of the table. “If a person did.”

“I'm asking whether someone did.”

He looks toward the cabinet, then back at you.

“Why after that?” he says.

You know what he means. The first thing you saw on returning was broken glass. Miriam's hand is still wrapped. You cannot get behind that moment by going more quickly through the events that followed it.

“I don't know.”

“If it's a chance to put things right...”

“I don't know why it doesn't begin where you would want it to.”

He takes his hands off the table. The answer came out more sharply than you intended. You could apologize for the tone and leave the words where they are. You could also let the words do the work you wanted, which was to stop him asking.

### Choice L1-G01

- **“Apologize for making his failure answer the question. Keep the possibility of a gift open.”**

  “I wasn't trying to defend the door,” he says. “I wanted to know whether you thought that hand was part of the arrangement.” You tell him you do not know that it was arranged at all. “Then say what you hope. I can hear that.” You say you hope an opportunity can be given without every harm around it being intended for its sake. He does not agree, but he leaves the question in that form.

- **“Say the timing is an objection to your hope, and ask what another explanation would need to account for.”**

  “The memory,” he says. “The place you arrived. What came with you, if anything did. Whether anybody else has had this happen. All of it.” You tell him you are not offering to go through the door merely so he can add another result. “I didn't ask that.” He looks at the paper. “I was about to.”

### Common passage

Miriam asks what you mean by God giving the opportunity. You say you do not mean someone hiding behind the service door. That makes her smile briefly. You are annoyed to have been funny without intending it.

“I mean that this could belong to an intention,” you say. “That I'm not the only thing in it which can want something.”

“I think you belong to a nature that can be understood,” she says. “And your wanting belongs to it. I don’t think that requires an intention outside it, choosing this weekend for you.”

“Why outside? Why couldn’t intention belong to the whole of it, the way you say thought does?”

“It’s a fair question. Your having purposes shows that purposes occur in nature. It doesn’t show that nature as a whole is reaching for something it lacks.”

“A person can give something without needing to get it back.”

“Yes,” she says. “Then we would need to examine that claim about giving, not treat your good fortune as proof of a missing intention. I haven’t answered it merely by saying outside.”

“That's what you think. Why is your way of saying it less hopeful than mine?”

“It may not be less hopeful. It makes a different claim. I think the activity of understanding is itself a real part of that nature's understanding. Not a message sent to us by something that stands apart from everything it has made.”

“Would you call that love?”

“Yes.”

“Then there is something more than finding the correct explanation.”

“Joy in understanding, too. And I mean something stronger than liking what I find. But I don't mean an affection which turns toward me because I have pleased it.”

“Ada could refuse,” you say. “She just did. I asked her to say my name again and she wouldn’t. If she takes my hand after that, the refusal is part of what makes it hers.”

Miriam looks toward the workshop.

“Does anything like that survive in what you call love?” you ask. “Or have you kept the word because it feels better than understanding?”

“I think understanding can itself be joyful, and that it can be an activity of the same nature understanding itself through a particular mind. That is what I mean by intellectual love. It isn’t Ada’s affection with the refusal taken out. I don’t think her affection can be replaced by it.”

“That’s the one I’m looking for,” you say.

“I heard you.”

“Would this include the other Ada?” you ask.

Miriam waits.

“The one who answered me before. If that happened, it happened to her too. Is she somewhere in your nature, or have I made her into something only I remember?”

“I don't know what this return has done,” Miriam says. “Calling it one nature doesn't tell me that.”

“But if your account is right, she can't just be nothing because I can't get back to her.”

“What happened would need an account. It wouldn't cease to need one because your present record was poor.”

Simon says there may not be two Adas. You tell him you know what she said, and that the woman in the workshop has not said it. He says he is not denying the difference. He wants to know what makes it a second person rather than a different history of the same one.

You find you do not want to win that argument. Either answer leaves someone beyond the conversation you are having now.

### Choice L1-G02

- **“Say you will treat the remembered encounter as something that mattered, even without knowing where it belongs.”**

  “Then don't make her repeat it for us,” Miriam says. You tell her that is not what you have agreed. You might ask Ada the same question. You will not say her new answer erases the first. Simon writes neither a tick nor a cross beside this. It is something you intend to do.

- **“Say the present Ada is the person who can answer now; refuse to call her a replacement.”**

  Miriam nods. “Then she can still give you an answer you haven’t heard.” You tell her that is what frightens you. She stays beside you. The workshop door is open, and Ada has started singing too softly for you to recognize the tune.

### Closing passage

Simon asks whether he can keep the paper where it is until you decide on another test. You say yes. He puts a clean cup on its corner.

The clean cup has a little water inside it. Simon pours it into the sink and puts it back on the paper.
