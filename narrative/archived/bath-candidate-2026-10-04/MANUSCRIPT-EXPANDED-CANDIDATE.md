## o0.arrival: The Shape of the Water

You have come for your mirror. At the shallow end of the saltwater bath, Simon Vane has propped its largest surviving piece against a pink swimming float. The float is a rabbit. Its two ears rise on either side of your reflection.

You lent him the mirror on Monday, with the usual apology for the damage in one corner. It was taller than any of you. He said the damaged corner would be outside the view. He was building something for the bath’s last open weekend, before the winter drain.

“I wanted you to see it before anyone told you,” he says. “Ada cut the cord.”

Ada March is sitting on an upturned bucket, holding a saucepan by its handle. “Yes.” She looks at him. “Tell the next bit.”

On the spectators’ bench, Miriam Verney has a bag of frozen peas folded around her hand. You have known her chiefly as the woman who can get frightened adults into the water without using the word frightened. Her shoes are neatly paired beneath her. One sock is wet.

“I asked to come out,” she says. “That is the next bit.”

The viewing cabinet stands beside the pool. It has black cloth walls, a narrow slit facing the high windows, and a rigid door carrying the mirror. The glass is still bolted to that door in pieces. A concrete pier, part of the old gallery above, stands within the door’s outward swing. Two black cord ends lie on the tiles.

Simon turns his phone face down. On its screen, before it disappears, you see a photograph of the broken mirror and his message to tomorrow’s visitors: “The experiment was deliberately destroyed.” Twelve people have read it.

“The sight worked,” he says. “Whatever else happened. I need that understood.”

Beyond the windows a laundry has hung a row of white overalls. One sleeve is inside out. There is enough daylight left to examine the cabinet. The bath closes when Ada finishes putting things away; she has stopped doing that.

“Blaise?” Simon says. He has been waiting for you to look at him. You have been watching your mouth between the rabbit’s ears. When you turn, the face leaves the glass before you have stopped expecting it to be there.

You know this room by its sounds: a wet foot leaving the tiles, a chair dragged under the gallery, the short return of a voice from the wall behind the diving board. Simon has made something here that he says can be seen without a person. You want to know what he means by that before he uses your presence to establish it.

Ada shifts the saucepan across her knees. Miriam takes one finger out from under the peas and puts it back. You put your own hands in your pockets. The cold glass has not touched them, but you keep checking that they are empty.

You can establish what happened to the mirror, ask what Simon thinks the sight established, and decide what account to give the people expecting to come tomorrow.

## o0.bench: One wet sock

Miriam is trying to remove her wet sock without using her swollen hand. Ada kneels, takes the sock at its heel and peels it off. Miriam does not thank her until Ada has stood up.

“That one has the good elastic,” Miriam says.

“You’ve told me.” Ada puts it over a rail.

“The other ones leave a trench. I can still see the trench when I go to bed.”

“Buy bigger socks.”

“Then there is sock in front of my toes. I swim to avoid having things in front of my toes.” Ada makes a small sound through her nose. Miriam looks satisfied by it.

When you sit down, Miriam shifts the peas onto a folded towel. “I don’t need somebody to take me home yet. I need a dry foot. That part is done.”

She describes the cabinet from inside. A shallow tray of water, a mirror, a slit of daylight. In the darkness the reflected stripe seemed to have no distance. She could not tell whether it was close enough to touch. Simon had asked her to let her eyes settle before speaking.

“I stayed longer than I liked. Then I pulled the ring. There was a proper click. I pushed. Nothing. I pushed with both hands. I said, ‘Let me out.’ I heard him say something. I said it again. Ada said, ‘Stand back if you can.’ Then there was a little noise, like a button coming off, and I was pushing into daylight.”

She lifts her good hand and makes an inadequate model of a door swinging. “The edge hit the pier. That was when the glass went. I thought it had gone into my face. It hadn’t.”

You ask whether she became unwell before she tried the door.

“I wanted to leave before I was frightened of being unable to leave. Write that in that order, please. I can’t tell you precisely what my heart was doing. Nobody measured it.”

She asks you to keep the cabinet empty until the exit is tested. Simon calls down to ask whether she could describe the sight for tomorrow’s visitors. “Later,” she tells him. “I’ll decide what I want to say.”

## o0.workshop: The missing alto

Ada carries the saucepan into a room that was once used for keeping swimming costumes dry. Somebody has written the names of musical parts on the old drying racks. The bath choir rehearses here because nobody minds if it sounds like a bath choir.

“I do second alto,” she says when you look. “Did. They’ve moved it to a room over the funeral shop. Too far on a Thursday.”

You ask whether she misses singing.

“I can sing here. It’s the coming in I miss. Someone says the wrong thing about the weather and you’re in.” She tips the pan toward you. Two potatoes have rolled together in a little butter. “Want one? Cold at the bottom. The cooker cuts out unless you keep your hand on the plug. I’m not fixing it until I’ve eaten.”

She sets a hook knife on the bench, safely closed. Its blade is meant for cutting tangled lane ropes without cutting the person inside them. Two short black cord ends lie beside it. Their fibres have been sliced cleanly across. The knots remain tied.

“I used that. This was round the whole box, across the door. You couldn’t see it well. Black on black.”

She demonstrates on the empty saucepan, holding a loose string around its middle. The handle moves freely. The string would not stop a handle. It would stop the lid if she had tied it over one.

“You heard her call?” you ask.

“Twice. I was here, looking for the bloody plug. First time I thought he was opening it. Second time I went out. She was pushing. Cloth moved, cord stayed put. I told her to stand back. Cut here.” She touches one end. “The door came out hard. Into the pier. I couldn’t get between them in time.”

On the bench is the printed plan Simon brought on Monday. It shows the door, the inside pull and the pier. There is no exterior binding in the plan. Ada has pencilled a note by the door: “Check swing before glass goes on.” There is no tick beside it.

“You didn’t check?”

“No. I told him to. Then I got on with the filter. That’s what I did.” She does not move the plan away from you.

## o0.gallery: The view for nobody

Simon has gone upstairs to fold the borrowed chairs. Miriam has followed him as far as the landing. She tells him one of his socks is inside out.

“It’s the soft side,” he says.

“It’s the label side.”

“The label has given up. It’s become soft.” He raises his trouser cuff. She looks despite herself. He asks whether she wants a chair; she tells him she has just climbed nineteen stairs to get away from chairs.

The high window makes the water below look darker than it is. Simon rests a folded chair on his shoe. He has built shop displays for eighteen years. You ask where he learned to silver glass.

“I didn’t. I learned to conceal the edges of the pieces people could afford. Whole sunsets with a strip missing. Nobody looks where you think they will. Once a woman stood behind the display and said she preferred it from there. I was furious for days. It was quite good from there.”

He shows you a clip recorded earlier, before Miriam entered. The cabinet is closed; a small camera, inside on its own, records a stripe of light reflected from the water into the mirror. The stripe appears to hang in depthless black. Simon walks past the exterior camera while the interior image continues.

“The first time, I thought it was something my eyes did. So I put the camera where my head had been. Same line. I left. Same line. Something continues without my attendance. You do see why that matters?”

Miriam says that a tide continues without his attendance too.

“Of course. But I hadn’t seen the separation so cleanly. A view with nobody in it. For a few minutes I stopped trying to put myself behind every event. Then I thought: perhaps this is what space is when we aren’t furnishing it.”

“You had furnished it with a water tray and a camera.”

“And I can subtract their defects. Different cameras, different trays. If the relations survive every change we can make, what reason have we to deny that those relations belong to the world itself?”

Miriam does not answer immediately. Downstairs Ada drops a chair, swears at it, and puts it upright.

“That is a stronger question than the card you wrote,” Miriam says. “The card says you have shown reality with the conditions of looking removed. A camera changes the conditions. It doesn’t remove every condition under which anything can be given to us.”

Simon opens a folded card. His phrase is indeed there: “An unconditioned sight.” Under it he has drawn the cabinet carefully enough to include the screws. “I want to know what would be sufficient,” he says. “Not merely to be told this isn’t.”

He will show the longer recording of the incident. You can also ask him directly about the cord or return later to repeat the optical result without anyone enclosed.

## o0.release: An empty test

Ada will not let anyone sit inside. She pads the mirror frame with folded towels and places a chair between the opening door and the concrete pier. Simon objects to the chair until he understands what it is preventing.

The original cord ends are kept aside. Simon has another length from the same spool. He puts it through the back loops and around the front of the cabinet, crossing the door at waist height.

“As you tied it before?” you ask.

“Yes. The cloth bellied away from the frame. Light came through the seam. I pulled it in with this.”

He has left the upper cloth panel unbuttoned so that you can see the latch. Ada gives you the brass ring through that opening. You pull. The latch withdraws. Simon pushes the door from the inside face with a broom. It moves against the cord and stops.

“Harder,” he says to himself. Ada keeps her hand on the frame. The broom bends a little. The door stays.

You release the ring and pull it again. The latch can return and withdraw perfectly while the cord holds the door. Ada asks Simon to stop pushing while you check the cable, then tells him to try again. The result is the same.

Ada slackens the knot. Simon pushes gently with the broom; the door opens until it meets the padded chair. Without the chair it would reach the pier. You compare the dent on the mirror frame with the pale chip above the old felt pad. The heights and exposed surfaces meet.

“I put the mirror on after you drew the arc,” Simon says to Ada.

“I know.”

“The old pad was too low.”

“Yes.”

“If I’d moved it...”

“Move it later. Answer this bit. Could she open that door?”

He pulls the unused end of cord through his hands. “Not while I had this round it.”

## o0.miriam_account: The person complaining

Miriam has arranged the frozen peas into a thinner layer. She asks you to hold the bag while she finds the opening. The peas are no longer frozen enough to remain individuals when she presses them.

You ask what she expected from Simon’s trial.

“A sight. A difficult conversation afterward. Perhaps an hour in which he didn’t have to sell anybody a bathroom. I did not expect him to prove what the world is.”

Why enter, if she rejected his claim?

“I didn’t reject the wish behind it. I once thought I lived at the point where all the causes stopped and I began. Work, Mother, the state of my knees: explanations for everything except the person complaining about them. As if she had been dropped in from elsewhere. That was a very laborious way to live.”

She puts the peas back over her hand. “I think my body and what I understand are in one nature. No extra little person in here who stands outside it and issues instructions. That is a claim about what there is. I do not mean merely that it is a helpful image.”

You ask whether a mind is then just a story somebody tells about a body.

“No. That makes thought the unreal part, or a decoration. What I feared had an order you could try to understand. What my body did had an order you could try to understand. I don’t think they are two strangers who met in the cabinet. I also don’t have a complete account of either.”

From downstairs Simon asks whether she had at any point wanted him to stop the light. “I wanted you to open the door,” she calls back. Her good hand has clenched around a pea. She notices and lets it go.

“I can speak quite calmly now because I’m sitting outside with my feet dry. Please don’t make that a test of whether I understand Nature. I was terrified. I may be terrified next time I have to use a lift.”
