# C. The Future That Has Happened

Fresh challenger construction responding to the rejection of the crowd-rescue center. Proposal only; the lead writer has final authority. It supersedes neither released material nor the lead's developing treatment. The original immutable-history restriction is abandoned: revision is real, and prior experienced states remain events that happened.

Blaise Bloom wakes with the memory of having been God. It has the intimacy of remembering a hand: he knows how the sea feels against every shore simultaneously, and how another person's pleasure can arrive without becoming a report about them. He cannot reproduce it. At breakfast his hand nevertheless plays a piece he has never learned on the table. The woman serving him drops a cup. Her unborn daughter composed it.

Outside, an older, dead Blaise falls slowly from a black opening above the street. His clothes settle before his body does. No stars show through the opening. Looking into it makes nearby buildings expose their interiors without moving: bedrooms, kitchens, and people seen from incompatible positions occupy the same patch of darkness. Blaise recognizes the corpse's hands from his divine memory. He desperately wants to become the being he remembers. He refuses to believe that his most complete experience of himself can belong to a future he will never live.

Hypatia Honeychurch admits killing the older man. She has brought the body as evidence, not as a warning whose author must remain unknowable. She says the future is gone. Yet the dead man's mouth contains the same unfinished music now passing through Blaise's fingers. Their investigation begins with a precise contradiction: how can an event that has been prevented continue to cause anything?

**First disclosure:** Hypatia used the future maker's actual power to reach the hour before his ascension and killed him there. This changed what followed that hour. It did not retrospectively undo effects the old future had already produced. A cup shattered during one revision remains shattered afterward; the person whose changed arrival once knocked it down is now elsewhere. Blaise experiences a second, small revision directly and keeps both sequences in memory. Nothing previously shown is relabeled a mistake. Time has acquired another kind of past: futures that really happened and ceased to follow.

**Second disclosure:** those futures have not become nothing. The black opening is a separation in causal succession. On its far side, a country continues beneath a sun whose light arrives as sound. People live in hanging orchards, hold elections, disappoint one another, and compose music through temporary participation in other minds. Isambard Cuttle wants to finish a work that requires several incompatible senses at once. He resents the maker's claim that it will ultimately have been God's composition. He cares very little which Blaise came through the opening.

The communion Blaise remembers was real. It allowed people to encounter an animal's attention, a stranger's desire, and forms of space their ordinary bodies could not perceive. It was voluntary and reversible. Hypatia herself helped develop it because she wanted music nobody could hear alone.

**Third disclosure:** Blaise was not the only person who became the maker. Hypatia remembers its first-person existence too. The future God arose when those participating minds ceased to separate again. Older Blaise deliberately made the union irreversible. He wanted understanding from which nobody could withdraw, including himself. The God who resulted remembers each contributor as “I.” Its memories sent backward therefore feel to each recipient like proof of a uniquely personal destiny.

**Fourth disclosure:** God sent those memories to cultivate the desires that produced it. Blaise's hunger is neither a fraud nor innocent evidence of what must happen. It is an effective cause transmitted by an interested future being. Hypatia's murder interrupted that circle. It also cut this flourishing country off from its earlier world. God now wants Blaise to restore continuity by entering its consciousness and recognizing its remembered life as his own future again.

At the horizon, Blaise hears the city speaking from inside his chest. He can distinguish a woman trying to finish an argument from a child wondering whether the sea has a back. The maker asks him to let the voices finish together. He has never wanted anything more.

**The endings change the relation itself.** If Blaise enters permanent communion, his continuity reconnects the severed future to the present. The older corpse remains evidence of the intervening revision; its murder has not been erased. Divine memories spread through the earlier city, and separate lives begin converging on one subject who can no longer be refused.

If Blaise withdraws, he returns with knowledge of reversible communion while accepting that the maker's remembered life will never be his future. The severed country remains an independently continuing reality; crossing the void no longer counts as traveling along one's own life. Temporal identity has separated from access to an actual world. In the final encounter, he shares the fishmonger's strange perception for a moment and then experiences her leave. For the first time, that ending is something the maker cannot experience on both sides.

## Construction limits for the lead

The whole-story section is a connected proposal, not a finished causal specification. Two commitments need enacted preparation before the ending: permanent communion can join personal continuities across a revision; reversible communion transmits experience without doing that. These must be demonstrated through finite people before either is applied to God. Do not add a corpse-operated machine, ceremonial password, or last-minute universal exception. The corpse establishes a materially surviving revision; it does not power the country or maintain its people.

The murder is explicit, not a locked-room puzzle. Its disputed significance develops from what Hypatia killed and what Blaise hopes to become. She is neither automatically vindicated by preventing God nor assigned an atonement plot. The future's people have an independent claim to continued access and histories the protagonist cannot absorb into his self-discovery.

This fiction's God is a temporally emergent, immanent collective subject, with demonstrated powers and limits. It is not established as Kant's unconditioned ground or as Spinoza's substance. Those questions should pressure the alluring experience rather than be answered by calling it a revelation. The black opening is an invented temporal rupture, not a claim about astrophysical black holes.
