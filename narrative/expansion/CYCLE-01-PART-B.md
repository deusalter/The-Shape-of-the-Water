## C1-S06: Four versions

Entry: Emmy has offered the source folder and the player has inspected the public invitation. Simon's presence does not automatically mean he sees private notebook entries. Miriam and Ruth are in the changing area. Ada is working at the lockers and enters only when called.

### Passage

The laptop asks Emmy whether she would like to finish installing something. She closes the question. It asks again in a smaller box. She moves that box into a corner of the screen, where it obscures the weather.

“I don't want to know the weather either,” she says.

There are four photographs in the folder. Two are of the cabinet from outside, one shows the light slit, and the last is a frame taken from the first empty recording. Emmy opens them in that order. The cabinet in the first picture has no mirror on its door yet. In the second, the mirror is mounted. Simon points out the difference before you ask. Emmy says she used the last picture because the outside of the cabinet looked like a place to store tables.

“That wasn't the final cloth,” he says.

“No. But it was the picture you sent.”

The last file opens across the whole screen. Its line has the small notch near the left. Below it you can see the water-tray rim; above it, the folded triangle of pale card. The picture has the same slight tilt as the earlier frame Simon showed you. Emmy opens the video he sent with it. The frame is there, between the moment his hand leaves the slit and the moment a ripple reaches the reflected light. She steps backward and forward through the interval. The triangle stays; the line wavers, then settles.

“This one,” she says. “That was the still. I didn't make the line.”

Simon says no one has said she did. She looks at you to see whether that is true.

You say you want to find out which picture became the invitation. She keeps her finger on the trackpad until you finish speaking, then opens the layout.

The picture on that page is level. Emmy clicks its edge. A frame appears around it, much larger than the part you can see. She pulls the page border aside. The tray rim and the pale triangle come back. The image itself has been turned slightly inside the rectangular opening. Nothing has moved separately within it.

“There,” she says. “Straightened. Then cropped.”

She takes her hand off the computer. Simon leans over her shoulder and she moves her head out of his way. He does not notice until he is already in the space she occupied.

“Could you turn it back?” you ask.

She finds the little box for the angle and sets it to zero. The line slopes again. With the cropping frame removed, the whole picture matches the exported frame. The pale triangle falls into the same corner; the tray rim lies at the same angle beneath the stripe. At the wider border you can see a blurred mark made by Simon's thumb as he moved the card.

Emmy returns the page to its saved state, then makes a copy before touching it again.

“I've lost things doing that,” she says. “You think you'll remember which one you changed.”

The invitation's dark area was already dark in the exported frame. Enlarged, it is not perfectly black. A faint part of the cabinet lining shows at one side. There is no new painted line in the page file, and Emmy has not raised a brush or eraser over it. The bright stripe, its notch and the dim lining belong to the same imported picture. You can see how they are carried together when she moves the picture within the crop.

Simon points to the wavering light in the video. “That's what I wanted people to see.”

“The picture isn't wavering,” Emmy says.

“It couldn't. It's a picture.”

“Then say which part of it was the invitation.”

He sits on the spare chair. Its back is against a cupboard door, which prevents him from leaning as far as he wants to. He moves it forward without getting up, making two short scrapes.

Emmy opens the message that accompanied the files. Simon wrote, “Use the one with the line. Lose the grey edges if you can.” Underneath, in a second message, is the sentence about the same horizon. Emmy's answer asks whether he wants the line straightened as well. His reply is a raised thumb. Then she sent the finished page. Beneath that, he wrote, “That one. Thank you.”

“You did ask,” he says.

“I know I did.”

“I mean I answered. I'm not saying I didn't.”

There is a long interval in which he seems to be listening to a reply that neither of you has made. Outside, the coin-box worker asks Ada whether she wants the old screws. She says she always wants the screws.

“I hadn't understood the edges would matter as evidence,” Simon says. “I wanted the picture clean. They made it look like something being tested.”

“It was something being tested,” you say.

“Yes.”

Emmy moves the page so the photograph occupies the left half of the screen. On the right is the text promising the same horizon. She reads it without moving her lips.

“At work, if I cut an empty chair out of a photograph of the waiting room, I'm not saying there was never a chair,” she says. “That's what I thought I was doing.”

“Would you cut out a step?” you ask.

“If the photograph was meant to show how someone gets in? No.” She looks at the chair where Ruth sat. “I didn't know there was a position to get into.”

Simon starts to explain that every picture has a point of view. She waits for him to finish, then points to the words.

“Then those shouldn't say wherever.”

He lifts the invitation and folds it along a crease it already has. The line disappears inside. For a moment all that faces you is the time and place.

### Decision C1-D07: ask about the approval

- **“Ask Simon what he understood when he approved the finished page.”**

  “That the edges had gone and the line was level,” he says. “I didn't open the page and compare angles. I liked it more than the first picture. I thought it showed the thing more clearly.” Emmy says nothing. He presses a thumbnail into the paper's crease. “It showed less of how we got it. I can see the difference now. I can't tell you I saw it then and chose it anyway.” You can record his approval and his present account separately. The message proves that he approved that page. It does not preserve the whole of his attention.

- **“Ask Emmy which instruction she believed she was carrying out.”**

  “Make a legible invitation. Make the picture fit. I thought he was checking what it meant.” She looks at Simon. “I checked the time because you wrote two times. I asked about the line because it looked crooked. I didn't ask whether wherever meant wherever.” Simon says he thought her questions meant she had understood the whole. “They meant I didn't understand the parts I asked about.” She gives you the message and page copy. Her explanation is an account from the person who made the layout, not an independent witness to his intention.

### Common passage

You ask to keep the received original, the editable page and the finished invitation together. Emmy copies them into a new folder. She adds the relevant messages as images, with the names and times visible. Before transferring it, she closes the other conversation that has opened behind them. You have not asked for her appointments, her son's arrangements or the rest of her correspondence.

The new folder is called “What was sent.” Simon asks whether that sounds accusatory. Emmy says it is what is in it.

Her son appears with the soft ball under his shirt. He says he has to be at his father's at one. She looks at the time and tells him she knows. He asks whether she remembers that his father is at the other house this week. She did not remember. She takes out her phone.

Simon begins describing a route across town. She shakes her head without stopping her message.

“I know where it is. I have to tell him where we are.”

## C1-S07: The shallow end

Entry: after the source-file work or before its factual submission. The player has permission to stay at the poolside, not to record Ruth's lesson. Miriam knows her own teaching arrangement and whatever has actually been said in her presence. She does not acquire the laptop finding merely because the player has it.

### Passage

Ruth has changed into a dark costume with a skirt attached. The skirt keeps rising when she enters the water. She pushes it down, looks at it rising again and tells Miriam she should have bought the plain one.

“It will get wet,” Miriam says.

“That's what the woman in the shop said. I meant while I was wearing it.”

Miriam asks whether you mind putting Ruth's glasses in the case on top of her bag. Ruth tells you which case. There are two. The empty one closes with a little snap; the other has a sewing needle stuck through a piece of felt. You put the glasses in the empty case and leave both on the bench.

At the shallow steps Ruth holds the rail with her left hand. Miriam is already in the water, her taped fingers lifted clear while she tests the next step with one foot. She tells Ruth the level will come to just below her ribs on the flat part. Ruth looks at the distance between her own shoulders and Miriam's.

“Your ribs.”

“About there.” Miriam points, without touching her. “Keep the rail until you've found it.”

Ruth descends. The skirt floats toward the surface once more. She lets it.

Miriam asks what she wants to try today. Ruth says they were doing breathing. Miriam asks whether she wants to do that again. Ruth looks toward the deeper water, where the lane rope crosses an empty rectangle of blue.

“I want to get to the other side without you telling me about breathing all the way.”

“How far across?”

“Across this bit.”

“Walking or swimming?”

Ruth gives her a patient look. Miriam accepts it and moves sideways so Ruth can stand where she had been standing.

They begin with Ruth holding the rail and lowering her mouth to the water. She comes up quickly, wiping her face. Miriam asks what happened.

“I went down.”

“What made you come up?”

“I didn't like being down.”

Miriam says they can start higher. Ruth asks whether that will help her get across. Neither is speaking crossly. The gap between them is small enough to hear.

Simon appears at the far end with a folded chair. He stops when he sees you sitting on the bench. You point to a dry place for the chair, away from the steps. He puts it there. He has come to watch the water and finds two people in it.

Ruth tries again. This time Miriam asks her to hum while her mouth is just below the surface. The sound breaks into bubbles. Ruth comes up laughing, then coughs because she has tried to laugh before lifting her mouth far enough.

“That wasn't what I meant to do,” she says.

“What did you mean?”

“The tune.”

“Which tune?”

Ruth hums the first few notes above water. Miriam does not recognize them. You do. It is a song that has been played in shops for years. The words you know do not fit the speed at which Ruth sings it.

“My daughter plays it while she's cooking,” Ruth says. “That's how I know when to come and complain about the smell.”

She lowers her face and hums once more. The bubbles interrupt the tune in a different place. Miriam asks her to keep one hand on the rail and let the other lie flat on the water. Ruth can feel it being pushed up. She presses harder, which sends a small sheet of water against her chest.

“Not a table,” she says.

“No. Let it meet you.”

Ruth looks up. “That's a very nice thing to say. What do I do?”

Miriam gives a short, embarrassed laugh. “Put your fingers together. A little lower. Like that.”

They practice until the movement becomes boring. Ruth says so. Miriam asks her to choose a point on the opposite wall. She chooses the cracked corner of a tile because she can still see it without her glasses. They agree she will lift her feet for one breath, with the rail within reach. Miriam stands to the side, leaving room for Ruth to put her feet down.

The first attempt ends before either foot has left the floor. On the second, one rises. On the third she floats briefly with both hands reaching forward, then stands and pushes water from her nose with the heel of her hand.

Simon has shifted his chair. Its leg makes a sharp sound on the tile.

Ruth turns. “Are you watching?”

He says he is. She waits.

“Would you rather I went?”

“Yes, for this.”

He gets up, takes the chair, and stops to retrieve the jacket he left over its back. The delay is ordinary and awkward. Ruth watches until he has gone behind the wall. She asks whether you are looking too. You say you can go.

### Decision C1-D08: the lesson's audience

- **“Leave Ruth to her lesson and wait in the changing passage.”**

  You take nothing from the bench except your own coat. In the passage, the splashes and voices are audible but their causes are hidden. Once Ruth laughs. Later she says, “No, I want another go.” You cannot see whether she crosses any distance. When she comes out, you can ask her if she wants to tell you.

- **“Ask whether she wants you to stay only to hold the rail-side towel.”**

  “I can get the towel,” she says. “It's the looking.” You leave it where she can reach it and step into the passage. A few minutes later Miriam comes to get a second one. She says Ruth has asked for a little more time. You have not been given a report of what happened while you were away.

### Common later passage

Ruth emerges with the towel wrapped around her shoulders. She has put her glasses on before drying the ends of her hair. Water has spotted one lens. She takes them off, wipes it with the dry corner of the towel, and sits on the bench where you left her bag.

“Three tiles,” she says.

You ask whether that was what she wanted.

“No. I wanted the whole way. Three's what I did.” She finds the paper sleeve and puts the invitation inside her paperback so it will not get wetter. “That's three more than I did last time.”

Miriam comes out behind her carrying a small float. Ruth tells her she still does not like the smell. Miriam says the smell will probably remain. Ruth asks whether next week is canceled when the bath closes. Miriam says they can use the municipal pool on Wednesday, but it will be noisier. Ruth asks what time. They work it out while she fastens her shoes.

When Ruth has gone to the entrance, Miriam stays beside the bench. She is looking at the little float. One end has a tooth mark, old enough that its edges have yellowed.

“I kept answering what I thought she needed to ask,” she says.

You tell her that Ruth did float.

“That's what she says. I saw it too. It doesn't make every word before it necessary.”

She lays the float on the windowsill to dry. Across the water, Simon has set his chair beside the open model. He is reading the invitation rather than using the camera.

She looks at Simon across the bath. “Then he has something. He has also told people they would get more than that.” She asks to see the comparison before making any public statement. The request remains possible; your account has informed her of the finding, not magically placed the source files in her hands.

  Miriam tells you where the municipal pool is. Ruth will have to change buses. She says she can meet her outside the first time, if they arrive together. Then she notices Ruth waiting at the entrance and calls across to ask whether that would be useful. Ruth says she knows the building. “Ask me again when we're inside. I always go through the wrong door.”

Exit: Ruth's three-tile distance is her report unless the source is explicitly Miriam's later corroboration. The player did not observe the private attempt. No private fear, medical diagnosis or family history has been disclosed by this scene. Refusing an audience has a real spatial effect and costs no evidence needed to solve the public-picture question.

## C1-S08: The question after the lesson

Entry: optional conversation with Miriam before the player leaves. This is not guarded by access to her first-night private story. If that story is closed, no new conversation silently reopens it. This encounter concerns today's lesson and her publicly stated philosophical commitment.

### Passage

Miriam hangs the towels without asking you to help. She uses her good hand to spread the first across two pegs, then has to trap its end against the line with her elbow while she reaches for another peg. You offer to hold it. She lets you.

“It's not very serious,” she says, looking at the taped fingers. “Which doesn't make it less in the way.”

You ask whether knowing why Ruth keeps coming up would make Miriam a better teacher.

“Some of it might. Some of it is her lungs being lungs. Some of it is being watched by a man she met twenty minutes ago. I can learn the first from a book. For the second, I have to listen to her.”

“Would that be the understanding you were talking about last night?”

“One part. It isn't all one kind of knowing.”

She has two pegs left and one large towel. She hangs it folded. Water drips from the low corner into the gutter.

“A rule about how bodies float can be true of Ruth and me and someone neither of us will meet. That doesn't tell me why she has come to this pool, this morning. It doesn't tell me what the effort is to her.”

“Then perhaps you can only collect more details.”

“I don't think so. Details can remain a pile. If I understand why one thing follows from another, I can do something with it when the picture changes. I know where to look for the mistake when it doesn't follow.”

You ask whether that is only a better way to be sure of herself.

“No. Being sure can be wonderfully cheap. I was sure about what to say to her. It didn't follow from what she was asking.”

She turns the remaining peg in her hand. A little rust has come off its spring onto the wood.

“But there's a difficulty in my answer,” she says. “If you ask how I recognize understanding, I keep having to show you some understanding. I can't put a certificate beside it which is easier to know and settles it for everyone.”

“Then Simon's certainty could make the same claim.”

“He can claim it. So can I. That doesn't make the claims equally good. We have to examine what we think we have understood. Not how loudly either of us feels it.”

### Decision C1-D10: press the positive claim

- **“Ask how understanding a rule could reach the nature of one particular person.”**

  “It doesn't simply grow into that because I've learned enough rules,” she says. “I don't have a completed account of Ruth. She would be right to be frightened if I said I did. What I mean by a more particular understanding would have to explain this life as an expression of the nature that makes it possible. Not replace her with a specimen called a human being.” You ask what would count as having done that. She looks at the entrance through which Ruth has gone. “More than I've done today. I can show you a change in what I understand about her. I can't ask you to accept the whole claim on the strength of three tiles.”

- **“Ask why she calls understanding love rather than comprehension.”**

  “Because I think there is joy in the activity itself, when it is understanding. Not a prize delivered after it. And if our thought is really an expression of the same infinite nature, that activity belongs to its understanding of itself. That's the difficult part of the claim. It isn't just my approving of the world.” You ask what loves what in that account. “Not an enormous person admiring a small one. And not me being rewarded for finding the right name for it. This mind's understanding would be a particular expression of that understanding. I know that sounds less like love than being loved by someone. I don't mean them to be interchangeable.”

### Common passage

“Why should belonging to it give your thought that reach?” you ask. “A wrong thought belongs too.”

“Yes. That's why belonging isn't enough. A confused idea is still real as something happening. It doesn't adequately explain its object. I'm claiming that in understanding we can know something through its causes, not only register what happens to strike us. You can refuse that claim. You can't refute it just by giving me an instance of being mistaken.”

“You can give me an instance of understanding without proving the infinite account.”

“I agree.” She puts the peg in the basket. “We keep getting to the part where I owe you another argument.”

The bath has become noisier without anyone raising a voice. Ada is rolling the empty coin box across the floor on a trolley. A corner of it catches in the grout and she has to change the angle. Miriam waits until the wheels are moving again.

“I don't want to use Ruth to settle this,” she says. “She came for a lesson. But I also don't want to pretend the thinking was something I did afterward, when the real work was over. Understanding her a little better changed the lesson while we were in it. I could stop repeating myself.”

You ask whether that made her free.

“A little less at the mercy of my first idea about what she needed. If you mean free of all the things that made me this teacher, no. I wouldn't know where that teacher was standing.”

She looks at her wet footprints beside yours. The heel of one has already dried away.

“Don't give him that for the new invitation,” she says.

### Exit choice C1-D11

- **“Keep her distinction in the notebook; ask for the next argument when there is time to examine it.”**

  “Then start with whether something can be understood through itself,” she says. “You can object before I get to Nature.” She has to leave to dry her hair. At the door she turns back. “You can object after that too.”

- **“Say the lesson changed what she did, but leaves you unconvinced about infinite understanding.”**

  “It leaves that to be argued,” she says. “I heard you.” She takes the basket of pegs with her. When Ada calls her name, she stops to answer the ordinary question about where the dry towels have gone.

Exit: both responses preserve access to later argument. Neither gives a philosophy-correct flag. The stronger positive intellectual-love claim is present and contestable. This scene is not an author certification that the finite lesson attained intuitive knowledge of Ruth's singular essence.

## C1-S09: The small mirror will do

Entry: after the morning's selected inquiry and lesson. The day can end with the publication question either submitted or awaiting evidence; no unsupported deduction is granted by departure. A further open trial has a concrete purpose: test the claim of position across specified observers and apparatus conditions, with Ruth's chair requirement included if she permits a future visit. Loan refusal remains effective.

### Passage

Emmy has shut the laptop and put her son's spare boots in a carrier bag. The ball has been returned to Ada, who puts it on top of the cupboard where he will be able to see it on his next visit. He notices. He does not ask whether he can take it home.

Simon holds the entrance door while Emmy checks that they have everything. They get as far as the first step before she remembers the lunch bag. He goes back for it. When he returns, she has remembered the other thing she meant to say.

“The chair,” she tells him.

“What chair?”

“When we were small. You tell that story as if I made you stand up because I wanted a speech.”

He looks past her at the boy, who has gone to inspect something stuck to the rail.

“You did want a speech.”

“I wanted them to look. They kept talking.”

“I know.”

“Do you? I liked the drawings.”

He holds the lunch bag between them for a moment too long. She takes it by the strap.

“Send me the new words when you know what they are,” she says. “I can change the page. It doesn't have to go out before I've read it this time.”

He says he owes her for the printing. She says she will add it up. He appears relieved that she does not say to forget it.

After they leave, Ada brings you the proposed cuts on the mirror. She has made a full-size paper outline of the smallest useful rectangle. You can lay it over the wrapping and see what would be lost. The old damaged corner lies outside it. Saving the rectangle would leave the frame too long.

She puts the outline down before asking who is doing the cutting.

“A glazier,” Simon says.

“And the edges?”

“A glazier,” he says again, with enough amusement that she leaves him alone.

You ask what he can pay now. He gives an amount. It is enough to make a beginning and too little to settle the whole loss. You ask whether that is all he has or all he thinks he owes. He tells you it is what he can pay before Tuesday. He has not yet received the price for a replacement of the size he borrowed.

“You could tell me the figure you want,” he says.

“You could get the price before asking me to settle.”

“Yes.” He puts the phone away. “I'll get two. Cutting what survives and replacing the glass. You can choose what you want done to yours before we decide what I owe.”

Ada tells him those are not necessarily the only costs. She does not volunteer an amount for her own time. He asks her to write down the hours she actually spent dismantling the unsafe cabinet. She says she can do that.

### Decision C1-D12: the immediate payment

- **“Accept the offered first payment as part payment, with the total still open.”**

  Simon writes “part payment” beside the amount and shows you before making the transfer. You keep the note with the loan photograph. Ada says Tuesday delivery still stands. Money has changed hands; the terms of the remaining work are still to be agreed.

- **“Wait for the two written prices before accepting money.”**

  Simon writes down the two requests he will make. He asks you not to mistake the delay for a withdrawal of his offer. You tell him you will judge that when he has the prices. Ada gives him the telephone number of the glazier who replaced the bath's entrance pane. He puts it next to the delivery address.

### Common passage

The small mirror is on the table, held in a wooden support. Beside it Simon has left three separate questions on cards. Where does the reflected line appear to be? From which positions can it be seen? What does the finished picture allow someone to infer? He has crossed out “the same” at the top, then written it again beneath the questions.

“I still think there's something there that isn't just a collection of viewpoints,” he says. “If you can show where it is from two places, the disagreement between the pictures is part of the evidence. It doesn't make the thing disappear.”

Miriam has returned in dry clothes. She asks how he will show where it is.

“Sight along it. Mark the positions. See whether they agree about where the image would be. I don't mean put a ruler on the image.”

Ada looks up. “Good. That would be a long afternoon.”

Miriam asks what would count as agreement. He takes a clean card for the answer. She sits down before he starts writing.

#### If the player has refused a second loan

He measures the width of his own mirror and enters it on the card. The large surviving piece stays wrapped on the bench with your name on it. When he needs the paper outline moved, he asks you to move it; he does not uncover the glass underneath.

#### If the player postponed the loan decision

He looks toward the wrapped glass, then measures the small mirror anyway. You have not given the larger piece a new use. The trial can be designed before another possession has to be offered to it.

### Final morning choice C1-D13

- **“Agree to help compare viewing positions at the next open trial.”**

  Simon asks which of the available times you can manage. You choose one. Miriam says she can be there for the beginning. Ada will open the room but has paid work afterward. Their names go beside their actual times. Nobody's name fills the whole afternoon.

- **“Arrange to return for the prices and delivery work; make no promise to take part in the trial.”**

  “You can still look at the record,” Simon says. “If you want to know what happened.” You tell him to keep the positions and the camera settings with it. He asks you to read the list before you go, in case the missing thing is obvious to somebody who will not be there. Looking at a list does not commit you to the trial. ### Closing passage

Outside, the coin-box worker has put the old mechanism in his van. Ada is keeping the screws in a paper cup. He offers her the cup as well. She takes it, then finds she already has the box she brought for them tucked under her arm.

The door sticks when you pull it closed. From inside, Ada calls to lift it. You do, and the key turns. Through the wired glass she raises the cup once, to show that everything is finally where it ought to be carried. You can still see the empty box under her arm.
