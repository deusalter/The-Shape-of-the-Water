**Latest owner decision, 2026-10-04:** retain **Blaise Bloom**. All other names below are reopened after the owner rejected the bath story and supporting names. Their researched provenance and former symbolic rationale are preserved as historical decisions; they do not constrain the next cast. No replacement names have been selected or owner-approved yet.

The owner clarified on 2026-10-03 that names must contribute symbolically, not merely have verifiable historical/literary provenance. The first checkpoint's sentence “Naming sources supply names only” was an inadequate interpretation and is superseded here. The three established names are retained through a fresh, explicit artistic choice; this document does not pretend their fully developed symbolism was already present in the earlier audition. Every named major character combines a historical figure's given name with a literary character's surname. The symbolic pair directs choices in the writing. It does not import a personality, profession, biography, death, racial identity or plot. Readers need no outside reference to solve the case, and no recognition reward unlocks evidence. Recognizing a reference can add a further reading of a scene that already works on its own.

## Blaise Bloom

Historical source: Blaise Pascal, verified in [MacTutor's biography](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Pascal/), successful HTTP 200. Direct philosophical writing inspected in [Pensées](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18269/18269-h/18269-h.htm), Gutenberg 18269, fragments 206, 277–279 and 347–348 in the fetched edition: the silence of infinite spaces, the relation of heart/faith and reason, and the finite “thinking reed.” Records: `sources/blaise-pascal-name.json` and `sources/pascal-pensees-name.json`.

Literary source: Leopold Bloom, directly named in Joyce's [Ulysses](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4300/4300-h/4300-h.htm), beginning of the fourth episode. Primary record: `sources/ulysses-name.json` and `sources/ulysses.txt`.

Intended work: the protagonist is a man confronting a repeatable world in which the continuity he feels may be invisible to everyone else. Pascal contributes the tension between a physically vulnerable consciousness and the immense reality it can think but not possess, plus the attraction of a God who addresses a person rather than merely being an impersonal totality. This is a pressure, not the character's assigned creed; his hope must answer objections. Bloom keeps this consciousness in a body, an ordinary day and particular encounters. The gesture he tries to recover matters because somebody touched this wrist, not because a universe has supplied a symbol. No Pascal biography, wager-as-game-rule, Joyce syntax or marriage plot is imported.

This is a new, deliberate male-protagonist selection after the owner's correction. The earlier Henrietta Leavitt/Bloom choice is preserved as superseded in `expansion/rejected/HENRIETTA-NAME.md`. It was never owner-approved and has not been quietly masculinized. No job, rent or workroom obligation drives the selected loop.

## Ada March

Historical source: Ada Lovelace, verified in the lead and infobox of [the preserved biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Lovelace), “Augusta Ada King.” Reinspection of `sources/ada-lovelace.txt` includes the discussion of applications beyond calculation, including music.

Literary source: Jo March, directly named in Alcott's [Little Women](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/514/514-h/514-h.htm), Gutenberg 514. Reinspection includes the sisters' theatrical rehearsal, domestic work and the tension between artistic desire and duties. Primary file: `sources/little-women.txt`.

Intended work: practical knowledge of a machine need not make a person the enemy of art. Ada can correctly expose the failed exit and still need the unproductive pleasure of arriving at choir. March contributes the question of whose artistic life survives the labour that keeps a household or room available to others. Ada's missed second-alto entrance and the eventual Thursday lift must therefore remain actual lived encounters, not a comic mechanic's reward for solving the door. A return must preserve Ada's artistic and practical life rather than reduce her to a repeatable response for the protagonist. No historical computing achievement or Alcott sister's individual biography is assigned to her.

## Simon Vane

Historical source: Simon Stevin, verified in [the preserved biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Stevin). Reinspected sections identify work on the weight of water, hydrostatics and perspective. Primary name record and normalized page: `sources/simon-stevin.txt`.

Literary source: Sibyl Vane, directly named in Wilde's [The Picture of Dorian Gray](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/174/174-h/174-h.htm), chapter IV: “Her name is Sibyl Vane.” Reinspected later exchange pits a living person's altered feeling against an audience's demand that she produce an effect. File: `sources/dorian-gray.txt`. The earliest audition used a secondary Harriet Vane locator; it remains in source history but is not the selected symbolic source. This is an explicit source reassignment without a display-name change.

Intended work: Stevin gives Simon's work a serious material interest in water, light and what can remain true across an altered arrangement. Vane directs attention to the danger of loving the effect a person or artwork produces while refusing what the person actually says. Simon's failure to hear Miriam's request as a request is therefore not explained away as mere technical clumsiness. His sister's eventual answer about the childhood chair can also release him from the belief that being seen requires a managed performance. He is neither Stevin in a bath nor a renamed doomed actress; his inquiry survives his failure, and no tragic literary fate is preassigned.

## Miriam Verney

Historical source: Zenzile Miriam Makeba, directly verified in the lead/infobox of [the preserved biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miriam_Makeba); reinspected music/public-voice material and the page's account of civil-rights activism. File: `sources/miriam-makeba.txt`.

Literary source: Lionel Verney, directly named in Mary Shelley's [The Last Man](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18247/18247-h/18247-h.htm). Reinspected ending passages distinguish the loss of Adrian and Clara from the abstraction of being alone, and show that writing about companions does not restore their living presence. File: `sources/last-man.txt`.

Intended work: a person heard in public still decides which private life is available to an audience. Miriam's command to leave the cabinet, her choice about describing the sight and her refusal to let a remembered confidence purchase present trust must have force. Verney contributes the irreducibility of particular attachments within a larger account of nature. Her grief cannot be dissolved by calling everything one substance, and Blaise cannot restore Miriam's earlier consent by accurately preserving her words. Makeba's exile, racial and political experience are not transplanted into this original character, and there is no claim that swim teaching is equivalent to her historical struggle.

L3 continuation, selected for development on 2026-10-04: the same name belongs to two separately encountered embodied continuations, with overlapping earlier history and different later experiences. Keeping it tests recognition and address without granting the first speaker sole authority over the name. Verney's survival-and-witness resonance now pressures the assumption that a surviving account recovers the people it remembers; Makeba's public/private voice distinction remains active when either woman refuses recording or a memory examination. This is a new deliberate extension of the name's work, not a claim that duplication was planned when the original character was named. Neither source's biography or plot is transplanted.

## Emmy Vane

Historical source: Emmy Amalie Noether, verified at [MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Noether_Emmy/); record `sources/emmy-noether-name.json`. The naming decision draws on Noether's work on invariants and transformations. Additional inspected invariant/transformation passages are retained in `sources/noether-symbolic-contexts.json`. No theorem is claimed to be demonstrated by the story.

Literary source: the same directly verified Vane family spelling from Wilde, intentionally shared with her brother. Her symbolic relation to it differs from Simon's.

Intended work: Emmy can change a presentation while preserving some relations and removing others. Her editable-page demonstration shows exactly what the rotated and cropped image carries together; it is not a fictional proof of Noether's theorem. As Simon's sister she is also an actual audience who can say what she meant by asking him to stand on the childhood chair. She liked the drawings and wanted other people to look, not a lifelong public performance from him. She has a dental-practice job and a son whose arrangements interrupt the inquiry; she is not a concealed mathematician or a benevolent explanation service.

## Ruth Garth

Historical source: Ruth Asawa, verified on [the artist estate's homepage](https://ruthasawa.com/), whose metadata identifies the Japanese American artist and her wire sculpture and arts-education work. Record: `sources/ruth-asawa-name.json`. The fetched homepage is evidence; failed guessed biography/Smithsonian URLs are not.

Literary source: Mary Garth, directly named in Eliot's [Middlemarch](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/145/145-h/145-h.htm), with a later inspected conversation recalling her refusal to burn Featherstone's will and rejecting the idea that she owes Fred an atoning marriage for its consequence. Primary file: `sources/middlemarch.txt`.

Intended work: making and learning occupy actual space and bodies. Ruth's ability to see the optical piece depends on where she can put her chair; her swimming lesson depends on what she can do and what she is asking. Asawa's reference gives material and educational participation weight without making Ruth an artist. Garth contributes a practical refusal to let another person's desired result assign her duty. Ruth can ask an audience to leave, report three tiles without pretending it is the whole pool, and still want to return. She must not become a uniformly wise visitor whose job is to pronounce judgment on the other cast.
