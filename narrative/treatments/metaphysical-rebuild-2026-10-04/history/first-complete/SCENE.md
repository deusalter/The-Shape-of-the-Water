# The man who remembers being God

Selected audition. Proposal only. The dead man is an older Blaise from a future which no longer follows from the present. The scene withholds that explanation until its investigation earns it.

Your body is lying in a room which has no outside.

Isambard has tried the other door. It brought him in through this one with his back to you. He is still carrying the cup he went to put down. The water in it has become black. He tips a little onto the floor and it falls upward, striking the underside of the table.

“Well,” he says. “I'm not drinking it.”

The dead man's feet are bare. One sock has been pushed into his mouth. You dislike him for this before you are frightened. Somebody ought to have taken it out.

Hypatia is sitting on the floor beside him. Her coat is wet from the elbows down. She has put her shoes neatly together under the chair, an arrangement which makes you angrier than the sock.

“Who is he?”

She looks at your feet.

“Don't do that. Look at me.”

“I am looking at you.”

You have heard the words in a thousand bad plays. They are no better here. You wait for the room to demonstrate them with a light, an alteration of scale, some vulgar assistance. Nothing happens. There is a dead man and an awkward woman and Isambard trying to clean the black water off his cuff.

Then you remember the sea.

You had put your hand into it and felt the weather on the other side of the world. Not heard about it. The cold was in your hand, but it was also the cold of a woman standing beside her orchard, angry because the fruit had set too early. You knew the shape of her anger from within. She knew your astonishment. Neither of you stopped being interested in the fruit.

You laughed together through two mouths. There were years of this. You knew how a bird could be tired of flying without wishing to land. You knew a man's hatred of you while he helped you carry a table. You were still yourself. More of yourself than you had ever been before.

The room returns so violently that you catch the edge of the table. The dead man's arm falls against yours.

“When was that?” you ask.

Hypatia says nothing.

“I was there.”

“Yes.”

“But I haven't been.”

“I know.”

Isambard comes round the table. He does not touch you. You wish he would, then are glad he hasn't. For a moment the wish itself feels small and private, a furtive thing you ought to open and show everybody. You remember knowing why there should be no such little locked places. You remember how kind that certainty felt.

The corpse has a scar across the palm. You look at your own hand. It is whole. A small satisfaction comes with this, so indecently quick that you almost laugh. Not me. The scar has settled it. You are ready to forgive the whole room for frightening you.

Then the memory continues. A broken glass. You had reached for somebody else's hand through the wrong side of it.

“Stop,” you say.

Hypatia flinches.

You did not mean her.

I know this man from the inside. That is the trouble. If somebody had brought me a likeness, I could ask who made it. If she had told me I would become him, I could refuse to believe her. But I have already loved being him. There is an orchard in my hand. I can remember wanting that woman to have enough warmth for her fruit, and I can remember the pleasure of being able to give it. Why must I pretend the pleasure was a warning? It was good. Whatever happened afterward, it was good.

You turn the dead hand over. The nails are short and dirty. You remember cleaning them before meeting someone important, discovering that you had become important enough not to bother, then being ashamed and cleaning them anyway.

It is worse than a prophecy. A prophecy could be wrong without taking anything away from you.

“Did I make that sea?”

“No,” Hypatia says. “You learned to reach it.”

“And the people?”

“They were there.”

“I remember them being inside me.”

“You were inside them too.”

Isambard puts his cup on the table. It passes through the wood and rests on a second table you had not seen, standing at right angles to this one. Beyond it, a street opens across the dark. People are lowering red awnings over baskets of white fruit. A child runs beneath them with a yellow bowl held over her head. She has no reason to look towards the room.

You can smell the fruit. You knew what it was called. The name hurts in its absence.

“They're still there,” you say.

Hypatia gets up too quickly and has to steady herself against the chair.

“Yes. I told you. I told you it wouldn't make them nothing.”

“You told who?”

Her hand goes to the dead man's shoulder. She takes it away.

You want to ask whether you were happy. You have just remembered happiness. You want a promise that it belonged to the right person, that no other Blaise can inherit it and leave you with this table. The demand is ludicrous and immense. You can feel yourself preparing an argument for it.

If I can be the effect of a life which will not happen, then what is doing the holding together? I do not mean whose hands. She will answer with a door, an instrument, another event. I will follow it. I want to follow it. But whatever she shows me, I shall still be the one asking why there is anything for it to do. The hunger does not end where an explanation begins. Perhaps it eats explanations. Perhaps that was what he mistook for God.

The dead man's mouth moves around the sock.

Isambard steps back. Hypatia does not.

You pull it free.

Your own voice comes out, hoarse and furious.

“Don't shut it. I haven't finished.”

The street turns. For a moment its afternoon is under your tongue. You taste the white fruit and begin to cry before you know whether you are afraid.
