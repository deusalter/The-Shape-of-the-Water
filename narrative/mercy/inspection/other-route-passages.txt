[a2.testimony-bounded] The account he can bear

“I kept the knowledge. People who had lived through those evenings had to meet me without it. I could prepare an answer to something they had not yet said.”

He described the bridge and the repaired bowl. He described the painted door which did not return because it had left the city. When he reached his own most recent request, he held the page farther from him.

“I wanted to meet Julian without that advantage. Vera remembered instead. I have learned what I asked from this account and from the people who were there.”

“Was that all you wanted?” Julian asked.

Blaise looked at him.

“No.”

He could still say the rest. The possibility remained present, like another person's hand held out in a room he was leaving.

“Some of the reasons concerned us,” he said.

“They concerned what you did to what I said.”

A chair creaked. Blaise wished the listener would stop shifting their weight.

“Yes,” he said.

Julian waited for another sentence. When it did not come, he folded the wet cloth into a small square.

Vera did not open the account for him. Blaise felt an unreasonable gratitude toward her, then wondered what she would have done if he had denied the words instead of omitting them.

He laid the pages on the table. Nothing in the paper had changed. The room had heard less than it contained.

[a2.forward] Time

“Let this evening continue,” Blaise said.

Erasmus waited. He seemed to expect a qualification that would improve the answer.

“You are not asking me to return it?”

“No.”

The patron touched the back of the chair. His hand rested there until a player came to retrieve the spare cloak beneath its short leg.

“You will have to help me move this,” Erasmus said.

Blaise lifted the chair. It was an awkward, ordinary weight. They set it against the wall, and Erasmus returned the cloak without looking at the player.

People began to leave. Some went to the road; some went home. A woman asked Erasmus when she could speak to him about a return of her own. He told her that they could talk tomorrow. Blaise heard the resentment in her answer. The city's wishes had not become unanimous merely because he had spoken.

Vera took her diagram from the door and found that one corner had stuck in the wet paint. She pulled too quickly and tore the cloth.

“Oh, damn it.”

Julian offered to help. She refused, then let him hold the door while she peeled off the rest.

When the company came for the scenery, Blaise took the horse's head. The others maneuvered its absurd length into the square. This time he could not know which leg would fail. He kept a hand beneath the nearest joint.

At the cart, Julian stopped beside him.

“I need some time,” Julian said. “I want you to let that mean time, without deciding in advance what I'll have done with it.”

Blaise nodded. He had more to say than that, but none of it needed to be said before the cart could move.

Julian climbed up beside the horse. He did not make their departure look like a rejection staged for the square. He checked the fastening, asked somebody to shift a box and waved to Vera. Then he looked back at Blaise.

“I'll write,” he said.

The cart went down the west road. Blaise remained until the part of the street he could see no longer contained it. Then he went back inside. There were cups to move before the room could be used again, and Erasmus had begun stacking them in a way that would certainly end badly.

[a2.letter] A reply

The letter began with a complaint about the horse. One of its legs had come off before they reached the rain.

Julian described where they had put the painted door and how the first rehearsal had gone. He had left a space, then begun again. He wanted a little longer before they met. He did not know how to make that sound less like punishment and had decided to write it plainly.

At the bottom was a correction to the judge's last speech. Blaise read it twice. He could see how to make the pause work.

Erasmus asked whether he wanted more bread.

“In a minute,” Blaise said.

He fetched a clean page. There was something about the judge he wanted to tell Julian, and something about the burnt toast, and an answer to the request which would be harder to write. He sat at the table and began.

[a3.good-morning] The good morning

“I want the morning,” Blaise said.

Julian put down his bag.

Erasmus looked at him, then at Vera. He did not ask either of them a question.

“And the memory?” he asked Blaise.

Blaise had thought this part would be easy. He had already done it once. But the man who had made that request had left a page for somebody to find. Here there would be only Erasmus.

“Keep it,” he said. “You keep it.”

Julian took a step toward the door. The room brightened before he reached it.

For an instant Blaise saw the wet cloth in the open bag, the uneven fold Julian had given it, and wanted absurdly to preserve that single thing. It had no special virtue. It was merely there, arranged by a hand which would not arrange it in quite that way again.

Then the light became the morning's light.

Blaise stood beside the horse. A length of string lay over his wrist. Julian was inside it, attempting to back out of a wardrobe which had ceased to contain him comfortably.

“I measured it,” Julian said.

“The wardrobe?”

“The head.”

Blaise waited.

“I forgot about the horse's other end.”

The laughter surprised him. He had been worried about the performance, about the robe, about the possibility of failing to give Julian the afternoon he wanted. For the moment he found nothing to calculate. Julian came out of the horse, took the scissors from beside Blaise's hand and kissed him.

Through the window, high above the square, somebody began beating a rug. Dust lifted into the light.

[a3.final-horse] The other end

The leg fell off when they tried to turn it.

Julian swore. Blaise laughed so hard that he had to set the head down. Neither of them was hurt.

“We could leave it here,” Blaise said.

“The horse?”

“We could let the audience come to it.”

Julian considered this with the seriousness of a man to whom several bad ideas had recently been useful.

“Help me put it back,” he said.

They lifted together.

On the other side of the square, Erasmus stood under the dining-room awning. He listened to the laughter until somebody came to ask him what it meant.

[a1.account-limited] The explanation he leaves

Blaise wrote on the lid of the box. The first sentence came easily.

I have found Julian outside Aubade. He is alive and has not returned through the edge. I asked Erasmus for the morning after he left. There have been other requests. I retained the knowledge of what happened between the restored mornings.

Now I am asking for a return in which Vera remembers instead. I want to discover how I will meet Julian without a stock of successful approaches. He remains outside. The return will not bring him into the city.

If I do not remember writing this, ask Vera what she saw and Erasmus what he did. Julian can describe what continued outside.

Vera read it.

“You said something else to me.”

“I don't want to put every feeling into it.”

“You want him to have the good explanation first.”

He did not deny it. Denying it would have required another sentence in the account.

She let him leave the page as it was. He added details of the unfinished roof, the stained door and Julian at the ladder, then watched her carry the original to the box outside. She returned with a copy for Erasmus.

Blaise was relieved that the omission remained possible. Then he thought of the man who would wake and discover that even the account prepared for him had been arranged to make one answer easier.

[a2.reading] His own voice

Blaise was in the theatre with a piece of string around his wrist. The horse and painted door were absent. A woman he knew as an astronomer stood in the doorway holding a page.

“You asked me to bring this,” Vera said.

He read the first sentence, then began again aloud in the pompous voice of the judge.

“I have found Julian outside Aubade.”

The solemnity improved the sentence. He went on, enjoying the idea of a man who had prepared this much explanation for an ordinary afternoon.

When he reached the request to stop possessing the knowledge with which the account had been written, the voice became difficult to continue.

“Is this a joke?”

“No.”

He knew the handwriting. That was not enough. A familiar script could carry a lie; a page could have been planted. Vera did not ask him to mistake recognition for proof. She told him what he had said to her, how they had gone to Erasmus, where she had kept the original, and where he stood while the light changed. She also described the first rescue at the bridge from the accounts her notebook retained, carefully distinguishing that older record from the request she had just witnessed.

“There was something you left out,” she said. “You told me you wanted to wake before you knew what Julian had answered. At the palace you also said you knew it would not make the answer never have happened.”

Blaise looked back at the page. Its account of surrendering an advantage was much easier to respect. The omission felt familiar in a way its history did not.

Blaise looked at the cupboard behind the missing door. He remembered Julian working here. He remembered loving him with none of the particular shame the page required him to recover.

“I don't feel like the person who wrote this.”

“You made an arrangement which might produce that feeling.”

It was a cruelly efficient answer. She seemed unhappy with its efficiency.

“I can show you where Julian is,” she said. “You needn't decide everything from this paper.”

Blaise folded the account once, then opened it again. He wanted to find a sentence proving that its author was a different sort of man. He kept finding reasons he could recognize.

[a2.public-meal] An interruption at dinner

Blaise read the account without the judge's voice.

At first the listeners assumed he was part of the meal Erasmus had prepared for them. He watched that assumption fail. A fork stopped halfway to somebody's mouth. A man at the far end began asking a question before Blaise had finished the paragraph which would have answered it.

He described the bridge and the fact that other returns followed. The document did not name the private arguments. He said that the document was his, but that the present memory with which he stood before them did not contain writing it. He named the people who could answer that gap with accounts of what they had actually seen.

“I chose to lose the knowledge,” he said. “I shouldn't make the result of that choice a proof that I was never involved.”

Erasmus remained at the table. He neither confirmed every sentence by nodding nor interrupted to correct the tone. Blaise found this restraint almost as difficult as contradiction.

Vera stepped beside him.

“There is something about the proposed test,” she said.

Blaise knew what was coming. She would repeat the words he had spoken about wanting to wake before Julian's refusal. The account had left that desire out. Vera had told him about it in the theatre; she had heard him say it before the return. He could leave the page on the table and claim he had supplied what was necessary.

[a2.public-interruption] Which question is permitted

“The question is what the return does,” Blaise said. “I've given an account of that. We don't need to examine every private motive in front of a room.”

Vera looked at him.

“You made a public claim about why you asked.”

“I've said enough to show the problem.”

“You have shown part of it.”

Erasmus reached for his glass and put it down without drinking. Blaise saw him recognize a familiar method of keeping an awkward question small.

Vera did not force her way into the next sentence. She stood beside the account and let Blaise experience the silence as something he had arranged.

A woman at the end of the table asked whether Erasmus could restore a coat which had been carried away. The ordinary question broke the silence. Blaise was grateful for it before he could prevent himself.

The inquiry went on with less of his explanation exposed than it might have contained.

[a2.purpose] A reason without an audience

“Suppose none of it was arranged for us,” Blaise said. “Suppose there is no answer waiting at the end which makes what happens worth it. Why should I do anything except make the next hour bearable?”

“You have reasons for wanting the hour bearable,” Vera said. “You have to ask what you are trying to preserve.”

“Myself.”

“An answer given very quickly for someone who has just arranged to forget part of himself.”

He disliked the joke. She noticed.

“That was unkind. I mean that your desire to continue is particular. It has a form. You wanted Julian's attention, a part in a play, someone who could tell you what your surviving meant. Understanding those desires can change what you can do through them.”

“Or just supply better means of getting what I already want.”

“Sometimes. Sometimes it changes what the wanted thing would be. You can stop needing an answer to prove that you were chosen and still want the person to answer.”

“And you can stop needing Erasmus to approve your diagram?”

She looked toward the patron. He was trying to work a dried piece of bread free from a plate.

“I can begin,” she said. “I haven't said knowledge immediately defeats every stronger desire. I would still enjoy it if he told me I was right.”

Blaise laughed. He had expected to be offered an improvement so complete that no actual person could fail to look mean beside it.

The page on the table would not change merely because they had found a larger account of it. He picked it up.

[a2.company-arrives] The cart comes back

The first thing Julian said was that the horse had become heavier.

“Wet,” said the woman pulling the cart. “It's wet.”

“I know why.”

She gave Blaise one of the handles before he could decide whether he had been invited to help. The cart was very heavy. One wheel clicked whenever the load shifted. He began listening for the click and found himself bracing for it even when the cart ran smoothly.

They brought it beneath the theatre window. Julian climbed down and looked toward the palace.

“Is she still in there?”

“Bringing back a plate.”

“Good. We need to start before she decides to bring an instrument as well.”

Vera came down the steps carrying neither. Her pleasure at seeing the cart was so unprotected that Blaise looked away. It occurred to him that he had rarely considered what Julian's arrival could mean to a person who did not want to be loved by him.

“Did you show them?” Julian asked her.

“Some of it. Blaise read the account first. When I tried to tell them what else he had said to me, he interrupted.”

Blaise kept his hands on the cart. Julian looked at him, then asked Vera whether she still wanted the door for her demonstration. She did.

The players began lifting things off the cart. One carried a bishop's costume with the sleeves tied together. Another had brought a pot of paint across the edge and seemed deeply annoyed that it had remained the same color.

“You knew what might happen if you came back,” Blaise said to Julian.

“Yes.”

“You could have waited outside.”

“I could. Vera has waited here for people to look at her work through rather a lot of mornings. I said we'd give her a room full of people who had come to look. And I want to play the piece before taking it further. These people know the jokes it started with.”

He rested a hand on the cart's side.

“I asked her what Erasmus promised. I asked the others if they wanted to come. One stayed at the rain. She says the whole place gives her a headache.”

“Is that all she said?”

“No. I shortened it.”

Blaise wanted to say that Julian was relying on him. That would be true in a particular, unwelcome way: the patron's promise still made Blaise's request consequential. It would also turn the risk Julian had considered with his companions into an offering made to Blaise. He helped lift the door instead.

Julian waited until they had set it inside.

“You are in the play, if you want the part.”

“Does somebody else know it?”

“Somebody else has learned it. You can be jealous later. At present she's got the other end of the horse.”

From inside the paper animal came a muffled request that they stop discussing professional questions and remove the head.
