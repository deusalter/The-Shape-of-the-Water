## The central action

Blaise's self-initiated surrender changes the investigation's subject. Before it, he knows things other people have lost. After it, other people and his own written account constrain a history he no longer remembers from within. Recognizing his handwriting is insufficient; the account directs him toward witnesses, the roof and Erasmus's admission. His next act is to read the account in public, where he can still choose to narrow what people hear.

The first and second requests are authored decisions by a particular protagonist. Their confirmation controls acknowledge consequential acts; they are not claims that the player was free to replace the whole plot with an unperformed refusal. Choices within the shared story govern what Blaise says, omits, asks and hears, the written account, the public inquiry and the final request. The optional disappearance finding records a supported fact. It is never a philosophy examination and never gates moral worth.

The second return does not make the player reread the first traversal. The player retains the transcript; restored Blaise must learn anew. Different actor identifiers prevent the engine from transferring his earlier notebook knowledge into that person automatically. These identifiers are representational bookkeeping, not authorial verdicts that a new soul has been created.

## Endings

Refusing the final return allows this interval to continue. Erasmus retains his power, inhabitants retain conflicting wishes, Julian leaves with his company, and Blaise begins another performance before learning what Julian's letter offers. The invitation texture depends on three concrete encounters: allowing Vera's public account, attending to Julian's independent project, and giving the fuller final testimony. Julian hears the public-meal history through Vera after returning; no absent scene is silently known to him. The invitation is still a visit with time requested, not automatic reconciliation.

Requesting the morning restores a genuinely affectionate earlier relationship while removing its later knowledge. The final laughter is neither fake nor a reward for the player's doctrine. Its cost is visible in the history the player retains. The ending is not a compulsory replay or a secret route to the other outcome.

## Literary decisions

The play gives Blaise something he actually enjoys making. Its comedy is not a demonstration that he has become virtuous. A substitute improves his part; the audience changes his timing; Julian can like the work without settling their relationship. The rope, bread, costume and horse recur as objects used differently, rather than as fixed translations of philosophical propositions.

The final judge scene develops from the opening's comic part. Blaise tries announcing that he has abandoned authority while continuing to direct everyone, then discovers another person's response can improve the act. His later public testimony remains capable of omission. The play has not purified him.

The faceless visual treatment does not erase embodied gesture, touch, appetite or differing vocal rhythms from the prose. Its block figures should not turn bodily description into clinical diagnosis or make every observed object untrustworthy. The literary setting includes curved streets, rising dust, reachable stars and standing rain as inhabited circumstances.

The philosophical exchange is deliberately sustained. The owner expressly asked for more philosophizing in Blaise's monologues. Some overlap in precise vocabulary is earned by a shared argument; Julian need not talk like Vera, and Erasmus can handle an objection through hospitality before he answers it. The exact anchors in `VOICE.md` preserve both formal reasoning and ordinary speech.
