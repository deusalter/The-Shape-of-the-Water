1. **Distinguish four orders without making the mystery indeterminate.** Blaise's apprehension, the city's objective sequence, Hypatia's biography, and causal dependence are different relations. The Second Analogy distinguishes apprehension from objective succession; it does not authorize every temporal topology. Its discussion of simultaneous causes also prevents the crude rule that every cause must precede its effect by a measurable interval: Kant distinguishes temporal order from elapsed time. B's genuinely backward interventions require their own fictional commitment. A repair is to make the passage's source event, destination event, and dependence recoverable while leaving the maker's biographical ordering initially unknown. The later hand injury then matters as evidence of a particular source stage, not proof that objective experience has dissolved. This supports a solvable murder amid an extraordinary reality. [K2, K6]

2. **Choice must constitute a difference, not merely reveal the only available answer.** In B, the previously heard mercy plus the stipulated future companionship threatens to make the climax an explanation of what must already happen. The new direction needs more. Shared observed events can constrain several possible complete causal structures without requiring several coexisting universes. A final act might determine whether the maker retains a separate commanding identity, whether creating becomes a relation among finite beings, or what dependence makes the already-heard act possible. These are alternative development options, not an instruction to install three endings. Crucially, compatibility with evidence is only epistemic until the fiction supplies a process by which Blaise's act makes one structure actual. The selected change must alter capacities, identities, or dependencies beyond his private interpretation. Preserve the voice, injury, murder, and witnessed miracles; do not silently equate an earlier inference about their ultimate source with the observation itself. If two endings share those events but differ only in who feels reconciled, the new request remains unmet.

3. **Make the conflict over agency positive on both sides.** Kant's Third Antinomy presses the demand for an unconditioned origin against the lawful unity of experience. His resolution distinguishes intelligible causality from empirical determination; it does not present time travel or a detectable causal gap as freedom. Its conclusion expressly proves neither freedom's actuality nor its real possibility. Spinoza I.28–29 requires finite causal determination and rejects contingency; III's adequate causation and V's intellectual love offer increased activity within that necessity. Thus a choice producing genuinely alternative realities under exactly identical complete causes would be a fictional departure from Spinoza, not his account of freedom. Conversely, a determined choice can materially cause a world without being a sham. Let the tempting offer be authorship exempt from dependence, and the competing positive offer be understanding and acting through the nature of particular beings. Hypatia's creativity and Blaise's fascination can become more active without disappearing. Intellectual love would concern reality understanding itself through finite activity, not merely treating a future deity more kindly. Whether that claim is warranted should remain a substantial argument. A finite repentant maker alone establishes neither Spinoza's God nor Kant's unconditioned. [K7, S4–S7]
