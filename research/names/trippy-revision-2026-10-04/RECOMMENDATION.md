# Naming proposal: Isambard Cuttle and Hypatia Honeychurch

Prepared 2026-10-05 UTC in the assigned 2026-10-04 revision folder. Proposal only. No live character, canon file, or game content has been renamed. Blaise Bloom remains fixed. The lead writer expressed provisional preference for the two recommendations below, not owner acceptance.

## Recommended replacements

**Julian Quince → Isambard Cuttle.** Isambard comes from the historical engineer **Isambard Kingdom Brunel**. Cuttle is the surname of **Captain Edward Cuttle**, Dickens's *Dombey and Son*. The BBC biography explicitly records Brunel's name and his audacious construction of bridges, tunnels, railways and ships. Dickens explicitly names Edward Cuttle; the inspected scene in which Cuttle prepares Florence for Walter's return combines affection, a rather badly controlled performance, and ordinary bread that he accidentally burns while attending to her reaction.

For this theatre-maker, Isambard makes an ambitious builder audible before he becomes somebody's beloved. Cuttle grounds the grand first name in a person who can care, make an occasion, misjudge its timing and surprise someone. The two sources give him useful opposing scales: improbable construction and a small anxious kindness. He need not inherit Brunel's career, Cuttle's seafaring, either man's manner of speech, or any source biography. In the existing bridge-collapse history, the engineering association should remain a quiet resonance, never evidence of a secret identity or a prediction. The clipped, slightly comic surname makes the unusually grand first name usable in ordinary affection. Approximate spoken form: **IZ-um-bard CUT-ul**. This is distinct from Blaise Bloom and Erasmus Rochester without borrowing a famous character's whole name.

**Vera Harlowe → Hypatia Honeychurch.** Hypatia comes from the historical Alexandrian mathematician and philosopher **Hypatia**. Honeychurch is **Lucy Honeychurch** in E. M. Forster's *A Room with a View*. The University of St Andrews MacTutor biography describes Hypatia's mathematical and philosophical teaching and reports surviving requests for her advice about an astrolabe and a hydroscope. Forster names Lucy and gives her a specific rejection of protective condescension: “I won’t be protected. I will choose for myself what is ladylike and right.”

For this astronomer, Hypatia connects thought to teaching, instruments and particular work. Honeychurch supplies a struggle to make one's own judgments while still being implicated in manners, admiration and self-deception. This helps keep her desire for recognition alive alongside her intellectual independence. The surname's audible honey/church sweetness offers an intentionally over-reassuring exterior for someone who is argumentative, vain and capable of disagreement. That sound association is our literary choice, not an etymological claim about Forster's intention. Approximate spoken form: **hy-PAY-shuh HUN-ee-church**. Use Hypatia naturally; do not invent an unsourced official nickname.

Hypatia's documented Neoplatonic teaching is not Spinozism. The proposed character may keep her present philosophical commitments; the name does not certify or prescribe them. The source's account of her historical murder is not a destiny to reenact, and its discussion of legendary beauty/modesty must not become an asserted biography or a saintly personality for this woman.

## Exactly five middle names

**Isambard Ernest Frank Christian Loveless Goodfellow Cuttle**

First name: Isambard. Middle names, exactly five: **Ernest / Frank / Christian / Loveless / Goodfellow**. Surname: Cuttle. Ordinary spoken and displayed name: **Isambard Cuttle**.

| Component | Actual source person or character | Evidence and intended tension |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isambard | Isambard Kingdom Brunel, historical engineer | BBC historical biography directly records the name. A maker's audacity. |
| Ernest | Jack/John Worthing, whose actual given name is established as Ernest at the end of Oscar Wilde's *The Importance of Being Earnest* | Act III supplies “Christian names, Ernest John,” followed by his recognition “Well, it is Ernest after all.” The play's invented identity finally collides with literal truth. It is not a name inferred merely from the title. |
| Frank | Frank Churchill, Jane Austen's *Emma* | Directly named in the novel; his later letter admits a “secret engagement.” The word sounds candid while the character has concealed his situation. |
| Christian | Christian, John Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress* | “My name is now Christian, but my name at the first was Graceless.” This is a character's explicit proper name, not an unattested added religious adjective. |
| Loveless | Loveless, husband of Amanda, John Vanbrugh's *The Relapse* | Listed in the dramatis personae. The audible absence of love jars against Christian and Goodfellow; the play also subjects his claimed reformation to renewed infidelity. He is **not** called Lord Loveless in the inspected source. |
| Goodfellow | Robin Goodfellow / Puck, Shakespeare's *A Midsummer Night's Dream* | Act II scene I names him and describes his mischievous disruptions; he acknowledges the identification. Apparent good fellowship includes unreliable theatrical interference. |
| Cuttle | Captain Edward Cuttle, Charles Dickens's *Dombey and Son* | Full name attested in the chapter XXXIX heading; an inspected later scene grounds tenderness and awkwardly staged surprise. |

The uninitiated ear hears **earnest, frank, Christian, loveless, good fellow**: a declaration of sincerity and goodness that contradicts itself before it reaches its surname. Readers who know the works get a second contradiction: several supposedly reassuring names belong to concealment, temptation and mischief. The five middle names are actual pieces of the proposed full name, not an initialism or a list of symbolic qualities loosely called names. They use the owner's newer historical/literary middle-name expansion; first and last still follow the established historical-given-name plus literary-surname pattern.

Use the full version once, in a practical embarrassment where someone has reason to say it. The scene should make a joke before it explains anything. A possible two-line texture, not canon:

> “Isambard Ernest Frank Christian Loveless Goodfellow Cuttle.”
>
> “No wonder you never agree with me. You haven't finished agreeing with yourself.”

The full name should never require a metaphysical registry, official identity apparatus, or a lengthy genealogy. The middle names do not establish that this affectionate man is secretly unfaithful, loveless or spiritually saved. Their contradiction is something he can live with and make use of, not a personality checklist.

All component provenance above was checked against actually retrieved public text, not search snippets or memory alone. **EXCERPTS.json** contains exact bounded excerpts, URLs and mechanically calculated line positions in the retained local text.
