# Naming and highly idiosyncratic voices
## Revision 3

The owner proposes names drawn from important historical figures and literary characters. The active working default below makes that preference operational. It is a naming method, not a locked cast. All four example combinations are auditions. Final selection belongs to the lead after the setting and relationships work.

## 1. Simple naming rule

Use a historical figure's ordinary given name and a literary character's surname. The new character is original, not the named historical person or a transported version of the source character.

Provisional examples:

| Candidate | Historical given-name source | Literary surname source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ada Sorel | Ada Lovelace | Julien Sorel, Stendhal's The Red and the Black |
| Blaise March | Blaise Pascal | March family, Louisa May Alcott's Little Women |
| Emmy Vane | Emmy Noether | Sibyl Vane, Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray |
| Michael Earnshaw | Michael Faraday | Earnshaw family, Emily Brontë's Wuthering Heights |

Historical source locations are S24–S27. Literary source editions are S28–S31. These are source attributions for the components, not character biographies. Confirm exact names in the primary literary text before locking the naming sheet. Avoid quoting modern adaptations or unverified summaries.

Prefer one short, stable display name in most conversations. Use full names only where the relationship or situation calls for one. Names should be distinguishable on a small screen and in memory. Do not expand into an artificial language, invented apostrophes, elaborate titles, symbolic compounds, or a roster of twenty alternatives.

No character must inherit the source person's occupation, conduct, philosophical position, gender, nationality, or narrative fate. A limited echo may be deliberate, but cannot substitute for characterization or provide the only clue to a secret. Do not name a doomed actress Vane simply because it makes her doom legible to a reader of Wilde.

Avoid famous detective surnames that advertise the genre, and direct name reuse from the reference game. Do not call the two main speakers Kant and Spinoza and turn them into doctrine mascots.

## 2. Cohesion and provenance

The final cast should sound plausible together in the chosen world. Apply the same provenance method within a suitable linguistic and cultural palette. The European examples above are a small audition bank, not a mandate for the setting or a universal naming standard. Do not assemble an arbitrary anthology of sacred, royal, scientific, and fictional names solely because each is individually interesting.

A concise `narrative/NAMES.md` records stable character ID, chosen full name, normal address forms, both sources, pronunciation when genuinely useful, deliberate allusion if any, and possible misleading associations. Mark audition, selected provisional, or locked. Lock the cast's names at story lock, then change them only by an explicit revision with a reference sweep.

Use stable nonsemantic IDs such as `char_001` in authored data. The surname is not a database key, a variable encoding guilt, or a puzzle answer. A display-name change must not break save references. Check near-duplicates and similar introductions, not only exact duplicates.

Do not assume an old source grants rights to an entire modern adaptation. Keep the new characterization and dialogue original. No rights clearance for the finished game or title is claimed by this packet.

## 3. What makes a voice idiosyncratic

A major character needs a distinctive way of attending to the world, pursuing a conversation, making an inference, and protecting something they do not want exposed. Lexicon and rhythm should follow from that activity.

Start with a short freewritten encounter. Derive the voice reference from successful passages afterward. Do not generate pages of adjectival instructions that the draft never obeys. The author-side reference should be compact enough to keep beside the scene.

Capture the following where relevant, preferably in a paragraph with examples rather than a giant questionnaire:

- Attention: the things they notice first, the details they miss, and what they insist is relevant.
- Inference: how they connect events, reach conclusions, revise them, or resist revision.
- Social action: whether they bargain, correct, entertain, deflect, reassure, command, flatter, or wait, and with whom this changes.
- Syntax and rhythm: sentence length, nesting, interruptions, self-repair, pronouns, degrees of directness, and how they end a turn.
- Figurative habits and lexical range: grounded in an actual life, without making every speaker's profession a compulsory metaphor generator.
- Withheld speech: what they cannot yet say, what they will not say in front of someone, and what their silence makes another person do.
- Register changes: ordinary ease, public performance, shame, intimacy, anger, fatigue, and the specific relationship that alters their speech.

These are writing aids, not psychometric claims or a diagnosis. Never infer a medical condition or caricature a protected identity to obtain a distinctive voice.

## 4. Hyperdistinct does not mean permanently loud

Some voices may be extravagant, syntactically difficult, obsessively exact, tangential, or abrasively funny. Others can be spare and unsettlingly direct. Quietness can be highly individual. The cast needs contrasts in amplitude as well as differences in vocabulary.

Allow idiosyncrasy to intensify or collapse under pressure. A flamboyant person can become almost plain when speaking to someone they cannot impress. An ordinarily concise person may explain too much when their explanation stops working.

Do not give every speaker the same neat final sentence, hard-boiled wit, rhetorical questions, traumatic backstory, or sudden profundity. Do not let every ordinary remark turn into a metaphor. Do not confuse an eccentric spelling with a lived voice.

The narrator and protagonist also need an attention pattern. The lead controls their continuity without using the narrator to correct every character's thinking.

## 5. Voice audition requirements

Use the final intended cast size, not a large audition population. The initial product still aims for roughly five to seven recurring major characters.

For each major character write three short, situationally different exchanges: an ordinary practical exchange, an exchange in which the person is trying to conceal or redirect something, and an exchange with someone whose opinion matters unusually much. Approximately 150–300 words per exchange is enough for an audition; expand when an actual scene warrants it.

At least one audition should include another cast member so voice emerges relationally. Then write one shared scene in which their rhythms interfere, cooperate, or change. The test is not whether isolated monologues have different vocabulary.

The lead selects and revises a few representative passages as the voice anchor. Preserve exact text and revision ID in `narrative/VOICE.md`; do not reduce it to adjectives during a handoff. Unused audition lines may remain unused. Examples in this packet are not a requirement to include a locked door, a bath, or these people in the final game.

## 6. Diagnostic reviews

### Name-stripped reading

After readers have been introduced to the cast, prepare about twelve balanced, anonymous extracts from ordinary and pressured conversations. Remove names, job titles, unique plot nouns, and catchphrases that trivially identify the speaker. Preserve sufficient conversational context to make each extract intelligible.

Ask a fresh reader which speaker they think it is, what textual features support the guess, and where attribution is uncertain. Include an unknown option. Inspect confusing pairs; do not assign an arbitrary pass percentage or pretend an automated classifier certifies character quality.

### Transfer test

Move an exchange provisionally into another character's mouth. Ask what would have to change in attention, social tactic, inference, and relationship for it to fit. If only a catchphrase changes, investigate the pair. A necessary ordinary sentence may legitimately fit several mouths.

### Relationship and pressure test

Read all conversations between a particular pair in sequence. Check how the relationship changes speech. Then compare the same character across listeners. A voice that never responds to anyone has become a costume.

### Anti-caricature and anti-aphorism test

Find an ordinary scene where each strongly stylized character can exist without performing their most salient mannerism. Inspect whether interruptions and unfinished thoughts are earned rather than decorative. Keep a genuinely effective closing line; vary scenes that all stop at the same quotable cadence.

Voice critics provide exact passages and proposed experiments, not a generic demand for more personality. The lead may reject a proposed normalization and preserve deliberate difficulty.

## 7. Research and voice must not become the same classification

The philosophical researchers do not own speakers. No mapping such as Ada = Kant, Blaise = Spinoza is permitted by default. The same person can think carefully about one problem and evade another. A character may misread a philosopher for a reason; mark that distinction in author notes without inserting an automatic correction into the narration.

Permit extended explicit argument when the characters have the knowledge and reason to pursue it. Make the argument's form belong to their relationship. Do not assign all abstraction to the educated character and all bodily detail to the supposedly simple one.

## 8. Conditions for locking a voice

A voice becomes a provisional anchor only after actual passages, at least one contrasting register, and a shared encounter have been reviewed. Record what works and what should not be imitated too literally. A lock is a baseline for subsequent development, not a ban on change.

At every substantial scene revision compare the candidate with that character's anchor and current relationship history. When an intentional change improves the work, update the anchor and explain which earlier occurrences are affected. Do not let a new chat overwrite a character because it prefers a smoother sentence.
