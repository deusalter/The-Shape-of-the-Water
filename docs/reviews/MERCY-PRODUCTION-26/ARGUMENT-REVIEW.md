## 1. Let Blaise distinguish his spectator-wish from his Kantian argument

At 74–76 Blaise distinguishes conditions of knowing an object from knowledge of it independent of those conditions. Vera subsequently calls her position “an answer to your assumption that an adequate understanding would require an impossible spectator” (81). He has not made that assumption in the argument. His personal wish for a witness who guarantees his meaning is real, but it is not the same claim.

This is a character's disputable reply, not an exact historical misquotation. The dramatic risk is that the scene lets Vera defeat the weaker picture just after Blaise carefully rejects it. His only response before it is “That is another claim which needs defending” (80).

Give him a brief substantive answer: objective knowledge under the conditions of human experience already counts as knowledge; belonging to reality does not by itself make thought knowledge of substance's essence independently of those conditions. He can recognize his own wish for an approving spectator without abandoning that objection. Vera can contest the restriction instead of granting it. This would preserve the actual disagreement between her identity claim and his account of objective warrant.

The inspected Second Analogy concerns the “formal conditions of empirical truth,” not a demand to compare appearances with an inaccessible original. Its distinction between subjective apprehension and objective occurrence should be assessed in the forthcoming surrender scene. Its absence here is not a missing-scene finding.

## 2. Give Blaise one affirmative attempt at the intellectual-love claim

The strong claim is genuinely present: “The mind's act is an expression of God's love of itself” (207). It is correctly distinguished from enjoying rehearsal or becoming less disagreeable (212–219), and the eternity objection concerns the particular thinker rather than a miraculous memory archive (221–229).

But Blaise mainly supplies objections while Vera states the possibility and concedes its outstanding proof. The earlier essence branch ends “You still owe me the distinction in this case.” / “I do” (109–110). The later exchange ends by retreating to an activity's intrinsic value while “the stronger account of blessedness” remains a further question (235). This is historically careful, but the strongest positive account does less dramatic work than its objections. It risks leaving Ethics V as the ambitious name for a possibility while ordinary performance supplies all the achieved good.

Let Blaise try the strong claim himself: if this particular act of understanding his desire is a finite expression of Nature's thinking, its value need not arrive as Erasmus's or Julian's endorsement. He should discover both what attracts him to that account and where the claimed identity exceeds the account he can presently justify. That is different from having him assent. A small practical consequence could be a revision to what he asks Julian to answer in their impending scene, or what he proposes to say with the demonstration. Lead selection remains open; the requested function is affirmative reasoning that changes an intended act, not an added doctrine quiz or mandatory new branch.

V.25 moves from adequate knowledge of attributes to a particular essence. V.32 makes the joy accompanied by the idea of God as cause; V.36 identifies the mind's intellectual love with God's self-love insofar as explained through this mind's essence. Those premises are stronger than pleasure in a useful activity. The scene should keep that ambition and its objection, rather than certify adequacy from a good rehearsal or add another disclaimer.

## What the module already achieves

Vera's substance argument distinguishes essential attributes from changing modes, then confronts the inference to necessary existence (58–72). It does not merely define everything as reality and announce immanence. Its compressed proof is vulnerable in the right place: being unable to supply an external cause is not silently allowed to establish an instantiated infinite substance. Her concession belongs to this performance of the argument; it is not a claim that the scene has disposed of every proof in Ethics I.

The thought/extension claim (79) is a substantial disputed identity claim, not a causal mechanism in which a spirit pushes matter. The invented return and memory laws receive no historical authorization from it. The source treats recurrence as an act within continuing reality, not discovery of a noumenal country.

The practical consequence is already clearest in `a2.reason`: Vera abandons a public claim to prove the patron not-God and offers observations that others may question. Blaise relinquishes his demand that the gathering pronounce them free. This is a real change to intended testimony, rather than a warning pasted onto an unchanged plan. The Paralogisms and intelligible-agency questions are also kept distinct: saying “I” does not disclose an indestructible bearer; empirical motives do not themselves settle whether reasons can claim an answer from him.

Vera wants credit, preserves several inadequate memories and resents the theatrical dog constellation. Erasmus turns an objection into an insult to hospitality and finds plates to stack. Blaise wants to soften an answer because a concession makes its speaker likable. These acts give the disagreement personal stakes and should survive any revision. No equal distribution of speeches or complete system exposition is needed.
