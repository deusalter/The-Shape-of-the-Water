Invitation route

The person who did it

Erasmus met them in the passage outside the dining room. Behind him, people were arguing pleasantly about where to sit.

“Did I ask?” Blaise said, holding up the page.

“Yes.”

“And you did what it says?”

“You asked for the morning and for Vera to remember instead. I restored it.”

“Did I understand what would happen to me?”

“You understood enough to leave that account.”

“There were other requests. What did I say they were for?”

“You wanted to have the conversations with Julian again. After he told you he wanted to leave.”

“Did I tell you that was when I asked?”

“Yes. More than once.”

Blaise looked down at the page. He had been ready to ask whether the timing could be proved. The person who heard the requests was answering him. He could question that testimony without pretending it had not been given.

Blaise looked at Vera. She had seen the request from beside him. Erasmus had heard it and acted. Julian had remained outside while the work there continued. The accounts did not become a view from beyond experience merely because they agreed. They did give him reasons to locate this morning after the act he did not remember.

“I don't feel guilty in the way the page seems to expect,” he said.

“I imagine that was part of the appeal,” Erasmus replied.

The patron could have made this sound like absolution. He had chosen an uglier kindness.

The diners were taking their seats. Blaise could have remained in the passage and allowed the confirmation to satisfy him privately. He went inside.

A place in the company

The cupboard behind the door had acquired the smell of wet paper. Julian hung the judge's robe on a nail and took a small bottle from his bag. He offered it to Blaise.

“For courage?”

“For the taste. Courage is unreliable.”

It tasted of orange peel and something burnt. Blaise drank too quickly and coughed. Julian took the bottle from him, pleased by the predictable result in a way Blaise found both affectionate and alarming.

They could still know small things about each other without possessing the whole account. A particular kind of drink made him cough; Julian found this funny and tried to hide how funny. These were causes. He did not want them removed. He wanted them to add up to a necessity which would prevent the person holding the bottle from leaving.

But how would they add up? No number of shared tastes could make Julian's future an obligation to repeat them. Blaise could imagine describing their affection so exactly that nothing about it remained mysterious. What frightened him was that the description might include its ending. He had confused intelligibility with a guarantee of continuance, then complained that causes made love cheap when they refused to offer the guarantee.

Julian put the bottle away.

“Vera told me you read the account aloud.”

“I used my ordinary voice.”

“Was it harder?”

“I think so.”

Blaise touched the robe on its nail. He wanted to ask a question whose answer would tell him what sort of evening this was going to be. Julian had taken out a loose button and was trying to decide whether it belonged to the robe or had simply arrived with it.

What the word claims

Vera was kneeling behind the scenery, trying to decide where the cloth could be fastened without covering the upper part of the painted sky. Blaise held one end. Julian had gone to find the other players.

“Higher,” she said.

He lifted it.

“I meant my end.”

She climbed onto a chair and made the fastening. For a minute they were quiet together. The room on the other side of the scenery contained the sound of people discovering where they had put things.

“You enjoyed that,” she said.

“The scene?”

“Yes.”

“I wanted him to enjoy it.”

“I know.”

“You aren't going to tell me that wanting spoiled it?”

Vera got down from the chair. “Do you imagine I have been arguing for a species of virtue without appetites?”

Blaise laughed. She was dusty, impatient and extremely pleased with the section of sky she had preserved.

“There is a form of love in that activity,” Vera said.

“Whose?”

“Ours. And, through it, God's.”

“I keep thinking you mean that one learns to admire the arrangement,” Blaise said.

“I mean something less comfortable than admiration.”

She brushed the dust from her knees and failed to improve them.

“Suppose you understand a particular existence through the attributes of what it depends on. Thought and extended reality are not two strangers. That finite understanding belongs to the same existence understood. The joy in that activity has its immanent cause within the understanding. That is why I call it intellectual love of God. The mind's act is an expression of God's love of itself.”

“A very large name for something happening behind a painted door.”

“Size isn't the claim.”

“Then let me make the objection properly.”

Blaise moved the chair aside. He wanted to stop being tempted to climb it merely because she had stood there while speaking.

“Understanding why Julian came back is one thing. I can be wrong about it. Understanding this particular existence through God's attributes sounds like something else. What justifies calling what we have done adequate in that sense?”

“The fact that you enjoyed a rehearsal doesn't.”

“Nor that I have become less disagreeable?”

“I would hesitate to build a proof upon that.”

He smiled, then waited.

“We have a true local account where we can show its dependence,” she said. “The stronger grasp of a singular essence is not achieved by adding the word God to that account. I am arguing that it is possible, and that it would be an activity of this existence rather than a glimpse permitted by an outside spectator. You asked why a world without that spectator needn't leave every understanding stranded.”

“Let me try to think what you're claiming,” Blaise said.

He rested the edge of the cloth on the chair. He had wanted Julian's laughter to prove something about the necessity of his place in Julian's life. The laugh was a real pleasure. The conclusion had been an extra demand, made so quickly that he had mistaken it for something the pleasure contained. He could follow that demand now without pretending he no longer wanted the laughter.

“When I understand how that desire works,” he said, “the understanding isn't an observer standing apart from me. It changes what I can want through the desire. On your account that is Nature thinking in this particular way, through this particular life.”

“Yes.”

“Then I don't have to make Julian's answer confer reality on the activity. If I understand, something real has happened in the understanding before he tells me whether he approves.”

“It has.”

Blaise felt an unexpected pleasure in putting the thought together. He had been asking for existence to face him and say that he belonged to it. Vera's claim was more intimate and gave him less of what he had asked for: this finite activity would be an expression of the existence he wanted to address. There would be no second face to turn toward him.

“I can see why you want to call the joy its own,” he said. “I still don't see how knowing that a thought belongs to reality makes this thought God's understanding of itself. I have understood a demand I was making. I haven't derived my particular essence from infinite thought.”

“No. Belonging alone doesn't make an idea adequate. I mean understanding the particular dependence through what makes it necessary, not simply noticing that you have a place in a very large whole. That is the stronger step I'm defending.”

“And the step I'm still asking you to establish.”

“Yes.”

He could not accept the whole identity merely because it relieved a fear. He could nevertheless attempt the understanding instead of asking an approving person to substitute for it. When he spoke after the play, he would have to say what he had learned and allow Julian's answer to concern that history. He had been preparing a question about whether a man who learned the truth deserved to be loved. He could hear the concealed request for a verdict now. He did not want to put that question into Julian's mouth.

“And you think that understanding is love,” he said.

“The joy referred to that immanent cause. Not somebody above the ceiling approving the performance. Not a reward handed back for believing the right thing.”

Blaise listened to Julian's voice through the scenery. He could not make out the words. The thought that this sound could be replaced by another morning still frightened him.

“If I understand a truth which doesn't grow old,” he said, “what happens to the one who understood it? You can't put eternity where my memory used to be and expect me to feel preserved.”

“I don't mean your memories will go on somewhere.”

“Then what do you mean by my mind's love?”

Vera took time before answering.

“That this particular mind's adequate activity is not made eternal by lasting a very long time. It is understood through the necessity of the existence on which it depends. I know how difficult it is to say what remains particular there. I won't offer you a little undamaged person waiting after the loss.”

“I don't want to lose it.”

“No.”

“Do you?”

“I am very fond of several quite inadequate memories.”

He had been preparing to accuse her of finding a consoling word for an annihilation she would not have to undergo. Her answer did not resolve the argument. It gave him back the person who was making it.

“Why should it be worth doing,” he asked, “if nobody can promise that the understanding keeps its owner?”

Vera rested a hand on the painted wood.

“Did you only enjoy making the scene because someone would later reward the man who made it?”

“No.”

“Then we have at least begun somewhere. The activity can be the good. Whether the stronger account of blessedness is true remains a further question. It need not be a prize for enduring this room properly.”

Julian called for Blaise from the other side. The room had become full enough that the voices no longer separated cleanly.

“Will you come to the company?” Blaise asked Vera.

“For a visit. I want to keep the outside observations going.”

“You could tell them where to put the stars.”

“They already asked. They want the constellation turned around because it looks more like a dog.”

“Will you let them?”

“It is their dog,” she said, with visible effort.

Blaise went to find his robe.

Someone beside him

“What do you lose?” Blaise asked.

“Your good opinion, apparently.”

“You already know I don't think you're God.”

Erasmus lifted a hand, then put it back on his knee.

“I know what you think I haven't proved.”

“Then what do you lose if I refuse?”

The patron looked toward the square. The people who had not found seats were listening through the open window. One of them had begun eating something from a paper bag. Erasmus had fed people there so often that Blaise could imagine him identifying the food by its sound.

“A person who knew what I had done and asked me to do it again,” Erasmus said.

“You have people who want it.”

“I was speaking about you.”

Blaise had prepared an answer about the city. It was difficult to use now.

“When I asked you to speak at the meal,” Erasmus said, “you said no. I took down the cloth. That was when I renewed the promise: while you remain in Aubade, I will not return the city without your request.”

“You allowed the refusal to stand.”

“I was very angry with you. I thought, for perhaps a minute, that I could have chosen a more grateful person.”

“Why didn't you?”

“I wanted you to be grateful.”

There it was, without the grandeur Blaise had expected. He could have laughed. The impulse hurt enough to stop him.

Erasmus watched his hands.

“And if I refuse?” Blaise asked.

“I shall have to experience your opinion for rather longer.”

“Will you keep the promise?”

“Yes.”

He said it with irritation. Blaise believed it more easily because the irritation had not been made into a lesson.
