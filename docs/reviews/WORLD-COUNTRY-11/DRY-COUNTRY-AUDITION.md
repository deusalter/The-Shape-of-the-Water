## Performed scene

The country below the room is dry enough to sound hollow under your feet.

Doorways stand on their sides. Some open upward, holding dust in the corners where people once caught their sleeves. A woman is hanging washing between two old lintels. The clothes have no wind to fill them. She flicks each shirt by the collar until it sits the way she wants.

Farther down, a row of houses has been built inside the spaces left by a row of larger houses. Smoke comes out through holes that were once meant for rain. A man on a low roof is lowering a saucepan to someone beneath him. They argue about whether he has put a spoon in it. He hauls it up, looks, and lowers it again without the spoon.

The woman walking with you stops at a broad doorway. She holds her painting against the upright, moves it a little higher, then takes it away.

“What?” you ask.

“Nothing. The blue.”

You wait. She has already gone on.

A loud crash comes from inside the next house.

“Too early,” a man says.

A smaller crash follows.

“Much too early. Now I haven't got anything left.”

You push the hanging cloth aside. Basil Boffin is standing on a low platform with a wooden tray under one arm. Seeds cover the floor. Behind him, three enormous pieces of an old room have been propped upright. Someone has painted a row of mountains across them, stopping wherever the old plaster has fallen away.

Basil looks relieved to see you.

“Would you do something for me before you start talking? Just stand there and say you've never been frightened in your life. Loudly. You can put your things down.”

“I've been frightened quite often,” you say.

“Yes, but say the other thing. I'm trying to have the storm interrupt you.”

The woman with you sets her picture on a bench, face outward. She keeps a hand on it until she has checked what else is leaning there.

“I've never been frightened in my life,” she says.

Basil shakes the tray. Nothing happens. He looks down at the seeds around his feet.

“I have rehearsed myself into a difficulty.”

You put the lamp on the bench and help gather the larger seeds. The little ones have fallen through gaps in the floor. Basil says those were the best part of the rain. He is speaking with such regret that the woman begins to laugh.

He laughs too, then asks whether the line sounded convincing. She tells him it was an excellent lie.

The performance is tomorrow. There are meant to be six people in it. One has lent her legs to a house move and promised to be back before the dance. Another has decided the part beneath his dignity, although he has kept the coat. Basil has learned all six speeches in case that helps. He cannot work out how to kiss himself at the end.

“You could leave that out,” you say.

“It's the only bit anybody has asked about.”

He puts the seeds in the tray and sets it flat this time.

“Dora, will you look at my mountains? I've gone too far with the white. From the last row they look as if somebody has been ill over them.”

She takes a step back. From there the small unpainted holes in the plaster do resemble missing pieces of sky.

“Stop before the edge,” she says. “You're trying to carry them round the corner.”

Basil turns to look. He lifts one end of the panel, considering a new position. The woman goes to help him.

Under the scenery is a floor you recognize.

Not the whole floor. A pale length beside a darker one; the place where a knot was cut out and patched; a groove worn by the foot of something heavy. You used to sit above that groove. A square is missing from the part beneath Dora's former place.

You come too close. Basil lets the panel down at once.

“It'll take your toe off. Give us room.”

The woman asks him to put it on the trestles instead. They turn it slowly and lower it with the unpainted side upward. You keep the lamp out of their way.

A recent saw cut runs through the old repair. At one corner the saw went too far and left a narrow extra slit. The inside of that slit is pale. Loose dust rests on the support underneath it.

You remember the repaired notch in the square below the supper table. Remembering it is enough to make you kneel. It is not enough to fit an absent piece into this hole.

“Where is the square?” you ask.

Basil wipes dust off his forearm.

“René took it. Why?”

The woman has put her hand on the far side of the opening. There is enough room between your hands for the missing piece.

“When?”

“Before I painted these. He helped me carry the floor in. I said he could have a bit from the broken end, because I needed that part straight for the sea. Then he wanted this bit, right in the middle.”

“You cut it for him?”

“He cut it. I held it. We were both very particular about different things.”

Basil points to a pencil line that ends short of the square.

“I wanted him to follow that. He wanted the repair left on it. He said the old notch would tell him where it belonged.”

The woman looks at you. You have both seen where the square was installed, but you do not have it with you. You tell Basil about the prepared place and the two supporting connections. You tell him that an earlier bodily arrangement appeared while a continuation remained in the orchard.

He looks first at her, then at the empty doorway behind you.

“Is she all right?”

“She could move the root when we left,” the woman says.

Basil starts another question and loses it. He rests one hand on the scenery. His thumb has left a wet mark in the mountain paint.

“He said it would be empty.”

“What would?”

“The place he was returning. I told him what I tell everyone who takes one of these. Keep it dry until you know what it's going to touch. Don't put it against a place someone's still using.”

“And he said it would be empty?”

“He said there wouldn't be anyone left in it.”

You ask whether those were his exact words. Basil shuts his eyes for a moment. When he opens them he looks annoyed with himself.

“The bit about nobody being left. I remember that. I don't remember whether he said ‘anyone’ or ‘nobody.’ I didn't write it down.”

The woman runs her thumb along the saw cut. She stops before the splintered corner.

“Where was I standing when this was a floor?”

Basil looks at the old doorway painted over by his mountains.

“If I've got it the right way round, you'd be where the hole is. But ask him. I only helped move it after it stopped being used.”

She takes her hand away. You offer to turn the whole panel so she can see it upright. She says she would rather keep looking at this side.

Basil brings over a strip of thin paper and a piece of charcoal. He does not hand them to you immediately.

“Are you going to want the whole thing?”

You had already imagined carrying it home. The entire old floor, with the absence in it. Something larger than anyone could talk around.

Behind Basil, the tray of seeds waits to become a storm. On the bench, the painting leans against a pair of dancing shoes.

**Choice: Ask to trace the missing square and its repair. Leave the scenery here until an actual comparison requires it.**

You hold the paper flat while Basil marks the corners and the extra saw slit. The woman checks the repaired grain on the back and asks for it to be drawn too. You write that René's removal is Basil's account, and that the fit has not yet been tested.

Basil pins another scrap over the mountain he smudged. He studies the new shape it makes, then paints straight across it.

**Alternative choice: Ask Basil to bring the panel to the supper room for a direct comparison, after he has found something else for the scenery. Offer to help him now.**

He goes out through the hanging cloth and comes back with a much narrower piece of ceiling.

“This was the sea. It can be mountains. The sea is going to have to be a noise.”

You help him lift the ceiling onto the trestles. He makes you wipe the charcoal off your fingers before touching the white paint. The old floor is set on dry blocks beside the doorway. None of its surfaces touches current responsive tissue.

You have an arrangement to make a comparison, not a memory of having made one.

## Shared continuation

While Basil puts his brush away, you find a short split pipe on the bench. Its two ends are different lengths. You know instruments made from these old chambers, though you have never learned to play one properly.

You hold it a little way from your mouth and whistle your one note into the opening. A rough second tone sounds beneath it. It lasts only while you keep blowing at the same angle.

The woman looks up from the back of her painting.

You try again. The sound is uglier this time, but unmistakably double.

For an instant you want to hide the pipe behind your hand. Then the shorter end rattles against your tooth and makes the intention ridiculous.

Basil reaches over and turns the pipe slightly. He doesn't touch your face. The low tone clears.

“There. That's a good noise.”

“It isn't the thing I used to do.”

“No? Would it come out of that end before?”

He leaves you to answer. The pipe smells of dry wood and somebody else's tobacco. It has done nothing to restore the little movement inside your cheek. You know exactly where the second tone is being made.

**Optional action: Ask to borrow the pipe and try it again outside.**

Basil tells you to take it. He has made two and this one is too loud for the room.

“Bring it tomorrow if you come. I might need a sea.”

**Optional action: Put the pipe down. Ask whether René took anything else.**

You set it back in the hollow its shape has made in the dust. You wait while Basil finishes cleaning the brush.

After either action, ask about the rest of René's visit.

“Three clean strips,” Basil says. “He takes those fairly often. They go round a curve without cracking. He said the person he was making them for kept trying to have a window open.”

“What person?”

“He didn't say. I told him to let them have a window. Then I thought perhaps that wasn't the problem.”

He goes to a stack by the dry outer wall and shows you what René took: thin material cut with the grain, flexible enough to bend around a bed or a root without pinching it. It is used all over the country. You cannot infer an age, a relationship, or a failing body from it.

“There's a house past the split trees,” he says. “I left some there once. Two roofs, one sitting low on the other. I didn't meet anybody. I was in a hurry to get the cart back.”

The woman lifts her painting from the bench. On its back she has found a loose curl of thread caught under a nail. She frees it without pulling the nail out.

“May I bring this tomorrow?” she asks Basil.

“To paint over?”

“To put where people can look at it.”

He looks around his room, already too full of scenery.

“Yes. We'll find somewhere.”

She carries it differently when you leave, with the face turned outward. At the hanging cloth, Basil calls you back.

For a moment you expect something else about René.

“Say the line once more. I've got the seeds in now.”
