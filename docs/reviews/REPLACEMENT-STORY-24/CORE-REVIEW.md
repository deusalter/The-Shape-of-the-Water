## Early judgment

This is a substantially stronger personal center: Blaise wants a particular love to mean more than its circumstances, and a real extraordinary power appears to guarantee that meaning. The desire can generate philosophical monologues that expose his interpretation of another person rather than explain fictional machinery. Retain that pressure. The present causal outline still risks becoming an obvious controlling-lover moral tale.

Especially protect the contradiction inside his appeal: he asks a powerful cause to guarantee that the love comes from outside causes. He can nearly recognize that contradiction, then find a personally persuasive excuse for another intervention. It remains alive after the patron's finite power is exposed; it need not depend on a final surprise about who was manipulating whom.

### 1. Make the relation more than the fact of captivity

As stated, the search discovers an escape and the climax asks whether to imprison the man again. That makes the revelation and the apparent correct response too easy to anticipate. Show why this relationship was desirable to both men and something the lover presently wants from Blaise, even while opposing him. A return initially sought for a shared purpose, later appropriated by Blaise, is one possible circumstance. A darker starting point can also work; the plot must then do more than unveil the abuse and offer moral improvement.

Give the lover a specific undertaking outside Blaise. His return for another person should follow that undertaking, not exist chiefly to inflict jealousy or demonstrate superior kindness. Let his affection, pride, loyalties or mistakes create actual difficulties for his own plan. His resistance to Blaise's idealization needs to be present before the final revelation supplies a new explanation of him.

### 2. Make the escape discovery cause a second problem

Specify what the lover did differently on this occasion, why it worked now, what the patron knew, and what Blaise does next because he discovers it. “Beyond the finite reach” should be one reversal, not an answer that simultaneously solves the disappearance, discredits the patron and exhausts the investigation. The next development must result from someone's consequential act. Adding an unrelated catastrophe to make the final choice look balanced would weaken the personal construction.

One concrete question for that account: if the earlier day included the lover inside, why does returning it not reproduce him there after he has escaped? Explain what or whom the patron can return in ordinary language. The lover's later voluntary re-entry could then restore the patron's opportunity. His independent reason for accepting that risk must be as clear as Blaise's reason for wanting him there.

### 3. Keep the difficult philosophy after the patron's claim fails

The patron needs an independent desire and a real continuing good supplied by the gift. Otherwise the city becomes a dressed-up prison and the mystery merely exposes a false God. Remembered occurrences explain the foreknowledge claim; they do not settle causality, freedom or the reality of love.

The stronger remaining question is what Blaise thinks would make a loving act real or free enough, even after he understands some of its causes. Let that understanding both increase his power to act and threaten his preferred interpretation. A climactic unpredictable act would not by itself prove uncaused freedom. Nor should showing a finite patron to be finite function as the whole argument about God.

These three points were sent directly to the lead. The full proposal may solve them through circumstances other than those suggested here. No verdict on its unwritten synopsis or sample is implied.
