My strongest impression is of someone trying to turn a remembered instance of care into something he can possess, verify, and recover. The uncanny events held my attention, but Blaise became more interesting when his need became inconvenient to other people. The final quarter contains the route's most convincing encounter: keeping time while Ada sings. It also contains his most revealing unattractive response: anger when Miriam will not repeat her testimony. The long philosophical exchange before it reduced my curiosity, and the return to bodily tasks and refusals restored it. I finished wanting to see what Blaise does when keeping his promise costs him an opportunity to cross the door. The excerpt ends before that cost arrives.

Blaise wants several things that do not quite agree. He wants evidence that his remembered Sunday happened. He wants to know whether the earlier Ada still exists. He wants the present Ada to provide the feeling he associates with her earlier touch. He also wants a larger witness who can keep that encounter from being lost. His claim, “There was someone who knew what I meant,” makes the personal need explicit. By the end, he can say “I want you to” when Ada asks whether she would get to keep the occasion herself. That is a meaningful change in the terms of his wish: her access to the experience has become something he must consider.

What he actually does is more revealing than what he says he wants. He says “Blue” to test whether he can depart from a remembered sequence. He reports his observations carefully, arranges a comparison of the wood, and leaves a suspect recollection out of the written record. He also asks Ada to repeat his name, sets up the chair so she might touch him again, solicits corroboration from Miriam, and requests that she repeat it after she already has. These are not all expressions of the same admirable investigative impulse. “You wait for her hand” exposes the chair experiment's concealed aim. Later, “She has the thing you need and it costs so little to say” exposes how quickly his distress becomes an entitlement to someone else's speech. That sentence gave me more access to him than several of his philosophical formulations did.

There is an emotionally important gap in his description of the lost encounter. The opening gives me ordinary, attentive care: Ada steadies a chair and says “There you are.” It also explicitly says, “You have no reason to preserve those three words.” After the return, Blaise speaks of reaching “a person I thought I'd lost.” I can believe that a tiny kindness becomes enormous when its shared history disappears. I do not yet see, within this excerpt, why this particular kindness establishes that Ada “knew what I meant.” The mismatch may make Blaise's projection interesting, but I cannot assume that is the intended reading. I was more persuaded by his need for the touch than by his claim about the uniquely understanding person it represented. Earlier material could supply a relationship I have not seen; this is a limit on how fully the supplied route earns that claim for me, not a claim that the larger work lacks it.

The final quarter works because it changes what repetition can mean. Earlier, Blaise wants a person to repeat a word, a touch, or an account so that he can recover a state he has lost. Ada's singing gives him repetition as a shared activity with room for timing and refusal. His early tap cannot lift her into the anticipated note. She lets an expected entrance pass and then chooses her own. “You have given her something to come in against” describes a relationship I have just heard happen. This is a substantial payoff to the opening discussion about recognizing another singer's breath. The tune does not merely illustrate a new idea; it allows Blaise to enjoy doing something with Ada that is not another test of her.

The confession after the song matters in relation to the preceding experience. Without it, the scene could become a very comfortable reward for learning to ask more politely. “I was frightened, and I liked it” makes his earlier privileged knowledge attractive to him as well as painful. Ada's question, “Did you want me to touch you ... or to find out whether you could get me to?” is exact, and “It changed while I was doing it” is a credible answer because it refuses a retrospective innocence. This increased my curiosity about him. I now want to know whether he will miss that advantage when he has promised to surrender some control.

I did feel a little friction around how promptly the confession yields intimacy. Her face becomes “interested in a way you did not arrange,” and the exchange soon reaches a promise she accepts. That can read as the successful route through a difficult conversation: admit the compromising motive and receive a more authentic connection. The text usefully resists making this a complete reward. Ada keeps his plain promise without allowing future complexity to dissolve it, and the anticipated song does not follow when she turns on the tap. Those withheld satisfactions matter. Still, the effect of his admission on her beyond this short encounter remains untested; I would not call it a demonstrated repair of trust.

My curiosity about the event itself—especially the Sunday change and what another crossing would do—remains more active than the ending's quietness might suggest.
