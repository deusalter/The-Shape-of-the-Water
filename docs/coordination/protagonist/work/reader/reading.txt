## 1

Ada has put two chairs beside the water. She sits on one and sets the saucepan on the other. You stand long enough for her to notice.

“You can move it,” she says.

You put the saucepan on the tiles and sit. The water has stopped reaching the lip of the bath. Each small wave turns back before it gets there. Under the gallery the sounds arrive twice, once from the thing that makes them and once from a direction where there is nothing to see.

Ada breathes in through her nose. There is a faint catch before she speaks.

“I know who's beside me by that,” she says.

“By what?”

“The breath before we come in. At choir. There's a woman I hardly know to talk to. I know when she's going to start.”

“Could you pick her out with your eyes shut?”

“If we were singing.”

“And if you weren't?”

“Then I'd ask her name like anyone else.”

You close your eyes. The dark behind the lids is reddish where the high window was. Ada moves one foot. You can hear the sole separate from the tile, but you cannot tell where she has put it down.

“Blaise.”

You open your eyes. She has moved the saucepan between your shoes.

“You'll kick that if you get up.”

You move it farther away. She watches you do it, then closes her own eyes for a moment. Her face does not look absent. It looks occupied by something to which you have no view.

You ask what she was thinking.

“Whether I put the potatoes away.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.” She opens her eyes. “Asked myself that twice now.”

Miriam calls from the changing passage that she has found her keys. You get up. The chair catches behind your knee; Ada steadies it with her foot. For a moment you are held between the chair, the floor and the hand she puts against your wrist. Then you stand clear and she lets go.

You say thanks. She says, “There you are.”

You have no reason to preserve those three words. You hear them and turn toward the service passage.

Simon asks you to get two pieces of wood from the long box under the workbench. He wants something to lay the wrapped glass on before he puts it into the car.

You want the changed words to be enough. For what, you cannot yet say. If you can do something different, perhaps you have not been returned in the same way as the glass. But you remember more now. That difference was already here when you came through the door.

“Blaise,” Miriam says.

Miriam asks you to set the phone down. There is a chair beside the bench. You put it there, screen upward. It goes dark. You wake it and read the date again.

You choose to describe the crossing and the vanished parcel, distinguishing what you saw from what you think it means.

He asks whether you saw the yard change. You say you saw the bins, then the entrance. You cannot provide the event between them. He writes that down without drawing an arrow.

Ada brings the saucepan to the sink. You follow her. She looks over her shoulder as you enter the workshop.

“Do you need something?”

There is the breath, the slight catch before the words. You have heard it often enough to be ashamed of wanting it to prove anything.

You ask her to say your name.

“Blaise.”

You hear it once, spoken by her. You had expected something to fit when she said it. Instead you notice how tired she sounds.

“Again?”

“No. What is it?”

You take a chair from the table and set it in the space behind your legs. Ada looks at the chair. You sit, then begin to stand, trying to catch the same edge behind your knee.

“What are you doing?”

“Just a moment.”

You rise slowly. The chair shifts. You wait for her hand.

She catches the back of the chair with both hands and moves it away from you.

“It's got a loose leg,” she says. “Use the other one.”

Your wrist hangs in the space where her hand had been. You can remember its exact warmth. At least, you thought you could until you looked at the empty place.

“I wasn't trying to fall.”

“Looked like a useful start.”

You laugh. It comes out as a short breath through your nose. Ada does not.

You choose to tell her about the touch and the words “There you are,” and ask whether she remembers either.

“Have I said that to you before?” she asks.

“Yes.”

“Before today?”

You cannot answer without choosing which kind of before she means. You tell her about the chairs by the water, the potatoes and the seventh key. She puts the saucepan under the tap. The water begins hitting its bottom, loud enough that you have to raise your voice.

She turns the tap off.

“I believe you remember it,” she says. “I don't. Don't make me remember by doing that with the chair.”

In the doorway, Simon says he has found a spare camera. He can leave it looking at the service passage if you want. Ada asks what it will look at when you are in here. He says nothing, then says that is part of the point.

You picture the side yard, the raised bin lid, the parcel hiding the glass. If you watch a record of yourself going through that door, there will be a man whose face other people recognize as yours. You will still have to say whether what happened to him happened to you.

“Will you come?” you ask Ada.

“To the door?”

You nod.

“I'll stand where you can see me. I haven't said I'm going through it.”

She gets up and places the faulty chair against the wall, back outward, so nobody can sit on it without moving it first.

Simon has put a second piece of paper beneath your prediction so it will not pick up moisture from the table. You had expected him to keep finding reasons to put it aside. Now you want him to stop looking at it.

The current result is written beneath the prediction, with the time and the people who saw it. It is a very small amount of paper for the way your stomach feels.

“I keep thinking I've been allowed,” you say.

Simon looks up. “Allowed what?”

“Another try.”

“By whom?”

You had hoped that could remain inside the word. You rub the spot on your thumb where the handle pressed it. It is no longer sore.

“God, if you want me to say it.”

Miriam says she did not know you thought about God. You tell her you did not know either, in this particular way. You have thought about punishment, and being grateful, and whether either requires someone to be listening. You have not usually said these things while someone else was waiting to get past you with a bucket.

Ada waits with the bucket. You move your chair. She passes behind it and says she will be in the workshop when there is something she can test.

“There was someone who knew what I meant,” you tell Miriam. “Now she doesn’t remember being that person. I’m still here, remembering her. I want to know whether I’ve been allowed to find her again.”

“Your remembering would have to be part of the account,” she answers. “So would her not remembering.”

“That's not what I mean.”

“I know.”

“Do you? If someone opened a door so I could reach a person I thought I’d lost, you wouldn’t leave the intention out of the explanation. You would ask who opened it.”

Simon folds his hands around the edge of the table. “If a person did.”

“I'm asking whether someone did.”

He looks toward the cabinet, then back at you.

“Why after that?” he says.

You know what he means. The first thing you saw on returning was broken glass. Miriam's hand is still wrapped. You cannot get behind that moment by going more quickly through the events that followed it.

“I don't know.”

“If it's a chance to put things right...”

“I don't know why it doesn't begin where you would want it to.”

He takes his hands off the table. The answer came out more sharply than you intended. You could apologize for the tone and leave the words where they are. You could also let the words do the work you wanted, which was to stop him asking.

You choose to apologize for making his failure answer the question, and keep the possibility of a gift open.

“I wasn't trying to defend the door,” he says. “I wanted to know whether you thought that hand was part of the arrangement.” You tell him you do not know that it was arranged at all. “Then say what you hope. I can hear that.” You say you hope an opportunity can be given without every harm around it being intended for its sake. He does not agree, but he leaves the question in that form.

Miriam asks what you mean by God giving the opportunity. You say you do not mean someone hiding behind the service door. That makes her smile briefly. You are annoyed to have been funny without intending it.

“I mean that this could belong to an intention,” you say. “That I'm not the only thing in it which can want something.”

“I think you belong to a nature that can be understood,” she says. “And your wanting belongs to it. I don’t think that requires an intention outside it, choosing this weekend for you.”

“Why outside? Why couldn’t intention belong to the whole of it, the way you say thought does?”

“It’s a fair question. Your having purposes shows that purposes occur in nature. It doesn’t show that nature as a whole is reaching for something it lacks.”

“A person can give something without needing to get it back.”

“Yes,” she says. “Then we would need to examine that claim about giving, not treat your good fortune as proof of a missing intention. I haven’t answered it merely by saying outside.”

“That's what you think. Why is your way of saying it less hopeful than mine?”

“It may not be less hopeful. It makes a different claim. I think the activity of understanding is itself a real part of that nature's understanding. Not a message sent to us by something that stands apart from everything it has made.”

“Would you call that love?”

“Yes.”

“Then there is something more than finding the correct explanation.”

“Joy in understanding, too. And I mean something stronger than liking what I find. But I don't mean an affection which turns toward me because I have pleased it.”

“Ada could refuse,” you say. “She just did. I asked her to say my name again and she wouldn’t. If she takes my hand after that, the refusal is part of what makes it hers.”

Miriam looks toward the workshop.

“Does anything like that survive in what you call love?” you ask. “Or have you kept the word because it feels better than understanding?”

“I think understanding can itself be joyful, and that it can be an activity of the same nature understanding itself through a particular mind. That is what I mean by intellectual love. It isn’t Ada’s affection with the refusal taken out. I don’t think her affection can be replaced by it.”

“That’s the one I’m looking for,” you say.

“I heard you.”

“Would this include the other Ada?” you ask.

Miriam waits.

“The one who answered me before. If that happened, it happened to her too. Is she somewhere in your nature, or have I made her into something only I remember?”

“I don't know what this return has done,” Miriam says. “Calling it one nature doesn't tell me that.”

“But if your account is right, she can't just be nothing because I can't get back to her.”

“What happened would need an account. It wouldn't cease to need one because your present record was poor.”

Simon says there may not be two Adas. You tell him you know what she said, and that the woman in the workshop has not said it. He says he is not denying the difference. He wants to know what makes it a second person rather than a different history of the same one.

You find you do not want to win that argument. Either answer leaves someone beyond the conversation you are having now.

You choose to say the present Ada is the person who can answer now, and refuse to call her a replacement.

Miriam nods. “Then she can still give you an answer you haven’t heard.” You tell her that is what frightens you. She stays beside you. The workshop door is open, and Ada has started singing too softly for you to recognize the tune.

Simon asks whether he can keep the paper where it is until you decide on another test. You say yes. He puts a clean cup on its corner.

The clean cup has a little water inside it. Simon pours it into the sink and puts it back on the paper.

It has. You want to ask again while it is still helping.

She lifts her coat. “I'm going to sit without this for a bit. I'm too hot.”

You sit at the end of the bench. The door in the changing passage remains shut. You cannot tell whether the other woman has made her call, or is looking at the number, or has put the phone away. Miriam rubs the seam in the side of her thumb. When she notices you noticing, she stops.

Available choices:

- Ask her to repeat only what she saw, so you can hold it apart from your own memory.
- Tell her that hearing the account helped, and leave her free to stop talking.

You choose the first.

“The chair caught your leg,” she says. “Ada stopped it. Her hand was here.” She touches her own wrist. “You stood up.”

“Again?”

“I've just told you.”

“I know. Hearing it is different.”

She looks down the passage. “I'm not going to keep saying it.”

You are angry with her before she has finished. She has the thing you need and it costs so little to say. The anger makes you hot behind the ears. You put your hand flat on the bench so she will not have to watch it shaking.

“All right.”

“You can ask me about something else.”

You ask whether she wants the window open. She says yes. You get up to open it and find you are grateful for the latch resisting your hand. You have to attend to it until it gives.

The changing door opens. The other Miriam comes back with her phone in her hand and puts it into her coat without offering an account of the call.

“I'd like something to eat.”

“There's bread, I think.”

“I'd like something that isn't bread.”

The woman by the window smiles. “You'll have to ask.”

They begin discussing what might be in the workshop. You know less about the cupboards than either of them. You stand, wait a moment to see whether you are needed, and go to find Ada.

Ada has found a potato in the saucepan. She cuts it in half with the edge of a spoon and eats the smaller piece. The other half remains against the side of the pan.

You stand in the workshop doorway. There is the sound chair; there is the one she turned to the wall. You put your hands on the doorframe to keep them away from both.

“Did you think I was lying?”

“About the chair?”

“Before she told you.”

“I thought you remembered it. I told you.”

“You did.”

She takes another bite. You had expected corroboration to change her face. Instead it has allowed her to go on eating. You watch the spoon scrape the curved bottom of the pan. In a moment she will turn it over to reach the bit beside the handle.

She does. Pleasure comes so quickly that you smile.

“What?”

“I knew you'd do that.”

She looks at the spoon. “There's potato there.”

You laugh. She scrapes harder, smiling into the pan. You have to let go of the doorframe because holding it has become ridiculous.

“I keep trying to make knowing feel like this,” you say.

“Like what?”

You cannot explain without spoiling it. She puts the spoon down and hums a low phrase while finding a cloth for the table. Halfway through she stops, then starts at a different pitch.

“Is that the choir?”

“It will be if I get it right.”

“Will you do it again?”

She looks at you.

“The tune,” you say. “I haven't heard it.”

“You wouldn't know if you had. That's how badly I'm doing it.”

She clears a space on the table with one movement of the cloth. “Tap there. Steady.”

You tap. She listens to three beats and shakes her head. You slow down. She takes a breath and begins beneath the sound of your fingers.

There is a place where you expect her to rise. She stays on the note. Your next tap comes early, as if your hand could lift her. She stops.

“Keep going.”

You tap again. She lets the next expected entrance pass without singing. You feel the unused breath in your own chest.

Then she comes in. You have not made it happen. You have given her something to come in against. For several bars you can hear where she is and where your hand will be without either of you having to catch the other out.

Simon appears at the door with a piece of wood in each hand. He waits. Ada finishes the phrase and waves him through to the other end of the workbench.

“Was that the right one?” he asks.

“It was a note.”

“There seemed to be several.”

“Don't overstate the achievement.”

He laughs and puts the strips down. Then he looks at the paper beneath them and stops smiling. He says he wants to measure the burned places while the light still reaches the bench. Ada tells him where the ruler is. He takes the paper and both strips to the concourse, beyond the closed workshop door.

Your hand is still tapping.

“You can stop,” Ada says.

You stop. There is nothing wrong with the silence that follows. That lasts until you think of the service door.

Available choices:

- Tell her that for a moment you liked being the only one who remembered.
- Tell her you are afraid of losing the way she has just learned to wait for your hand.

You choose the first.

“When you asked what I was doing with the chair,” you say, “I could have told you almost anything. You had no way to know what I wanted from it.”

“I had a fair idea you wanted to fall over.”

“I liked it before that. Coming in knowing what people would say. I was frightened, and I liked it.”

Her eyes move to the door. You let her look. You have supplied a reason she might prefer to have it open.

“Did you want me to touch you,” she asks, “or to find out whether you could get me to?”

“It changed while I was doing it.”

She thinks about that. You wish you had given her a cleaner answer, but her face has become interested in a way you did not arrange.

“Don't set the chair up again.”

“I won't.”

“I'm keeping that.”

You look up.

“What you just said. If you decide you meant something more complicated tomorrow, you can explain it then.”

“I want you to be able to.”

She puts the spoon into the sink. “Good.”

“If someone is doing this,” you say, “I want them to know we did that.”

“Simon heard.”

“I mean God.”

She rubs at a spot on the table. It was in the wood before either of you sat there.

“Would it be any better?” she asks.

“If somebody could keep it from being lost.”

“Would I get to keep it?”

You start to say that is what you mean. You are not sure it was. You had imagined a witness who could keep the whole occasion safe even if Ada had no access to it.

“I want you to,” you say.

“Then tell me before you try the door.”

“I will.”

She gets up to rinse the spoon. When she turns on the tap you hear the note she could begin against it. You wait. She rinses the pan as well. No song follows.

The two women have come to the workshop door. The one carrying the peas asks Ada whether there is cheese. Ada opens a small container, smells it and says yes. The other Miriam follows her to the sink to ask for a clean cloth.

You can leave what you said with Ada. It will still have happened. If you try the service door again, the woman who remained outside will not be able to repeat it merely because you remember telling someone.

Available choices:

- Keep the conversation with Ada private.
- Tell both Miriams and Simon that you have promised Ada to tell her before any further crossing; keep the rest private.

You choose the second.

You ask Simon to come back from the concourse. You wait until he has put the strips down. Then you tell all three what you have promised.

“I heard him,” Ada says.

The Miriam who came in wearing the coat asks whether you want her to remind you later.

“If we can speak later. Yes.”

“I can do that. I can't remind her for you.”

You say you understand. The other woman breaks a piece of bread in half and offers it across the table. Both take some cheese before Simon sits down.
