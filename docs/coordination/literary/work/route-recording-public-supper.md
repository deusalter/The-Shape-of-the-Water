## arrival / arrival.base

You have come for your mirror. At the shallow end of the saltwater bath, Simon Vane has propped its largest surviving piece against a pink swimming float. The float is a rabbit. Its two ears rise on either side of your reflection.

You lent him the mirror on Monday, with the usual apology for the damage in one corner. It was taller than any of you. He said the damaged corner would be outside the view. He was building something for the bath’s last open weekend, before the winter drain.

“I wanted you to see it before anyone told you,” he says. “Ada cut the cord.”

Ada March is sitting on an upturned bucket, holding a saucepan by its handle. “Yes.” She looks at him. “Tell the next bit.”

On the spectators’ bench, Miriam Verney has a bag of frozen peas folded around her hand. You have known her chiefly as the woman who can get frightened adults into the water without using the word frightened. Her shoes are neatly paired beneath her. One sock is wet.

“I asked to come out,” she says. “That is the next bit.”

The viewing cabinet stands beside the pool. It has black cloth walls, a narrow slit facing the high windows, and a rigid door carrying the mirror. The glass is still bolted to that door in pieces. A concrete pier, part of the old gallery above, stands within the door’s outward swing. Two black cord ends lie on the tiles.

Simon turns his phone face down. On its screen, before it disappears, you see a photograph of the broken mirror and his message to tomorrow’s visitors: “The experiment was deliberately destroyed.” Twelve people have read it.

“The sight worked,” he says. “Whatever else happened. I need that understood.”

Beyond the windows a laundry has hung a row of white overalls. One sleeve is inside out. There is enough daylight left to examine the cabinet. The bath closes when Ada finishes putting things away; she has stopped doing that.

“Blaise?” Simon says. He has been waiting for you to look at him. You have been watching your mouth between the rabbit’s ears. When you turn, the face leaves the glass before you have stopped expecting it to be there.

You know this room by its sounds: a wet foot leaving the tiles, a chair dragged under the gallery, the short return of a voice from the wall behind the diving board. Simon has made something here that he says can be seen without a person. You want to know what he means by that before he uses your presence to establish it.

Ada shifts the saucepan across her knees. Miriam takes one finger out from under the peas and puts it back. You put your own hands in your pockets. The cold glass has not touched them, but you keep checking that they are empty.

You can establish what happened to the mirror, ask what Simon thinks the sight established, and decide what account to give the people expecting to come tomorrow.

## cabinet / cabinet.base

The mirror is bolted to the inside of the cabinet’s rigid door, facing the shallow water tray when the door is shut. The door opens outward. A chalk arc on the tiles marks its swing. The concrete gallery pier interrupts the arc before the door can lie flat against the cabinet wall.

An old felt pad is screwed to the pier at shoulder height. The mirror’s frame reaches above it. At that higher point, a fresh pale chip interrupts the pier’s grey surface. White grit is caught in a dent on the mirror frame. You can compare the heights without moving anything.

The inside release is a brass ring on a cable. You can reach it through the torn cloth while standing outside. You pull the ring. Something clicks. The latch withdraws and the door moves a little.

“Working,” Simon says. He has come down from the gallery without your noticing. He sounds relieved by the discovery.

“Yes,” Miriam calls from the bench. “That is exactly what I heard.”

The latch is not broken. The two severed cord ends hang from loops at the back of the cabinet. At the front, where the cord used to cross the door, a dark horizontal groove interrupts the cloth’s fine dust. The cloth does not tell you who tied it or when.

Inside, a narrow stripe of daylight lies across the remaining glass. As water in the tray settles, the stripe steadies. It is visible from the open door. You have not yet repeated Simon’s full arrangement or seen his recording.

Simon puts his hand on the door edge, then takes it away from a sliver of glass. “There was an inside release. Please don’t let that get lost.”

## concourse / concourse.base

The bath is still open to the people already inside it. Ada has turned off the lane clock. The ordinary clock above the entrance continues, a minute slower than the one on Simon’s phone.

You have room to compare their accounts. The cabinet can be examined from outside. Nobody needs to enter it again. Miriam is on the bench; Simon is in the gallery; Ada has left the workshop door open.

You can compare the accounts of the cut and the request to leave, and examine what held or released the door. The object can show how its parts work. What somebody heard has to come from an account or a recording.

## gallery / gallery.base

Simon has gone upstairs to fold the borrowed chairs. Miriam has followed him as far as the landing. She tells him one of his socks is inside out.

“It’s the soft side,” he says.

“It’s the label side.”

“The label has given up. It’s become soft.” He raises his trouser cuff. She looks despite herself. He asks whether she wants a chair; she tells him she has just climbed nineteen stairs to get away from chairs.

The high window makes the water below look darker than it is. Simon rests a folded chair on his shoe. He has built shop displays for eighteen years. You ask where he learned to silver glass.

“I didn’t. I learned to conceal the edges of the pieces people could afford. Whole sunsets with a strip missing. Nobody looks where you think they will. Once a woman stood behind the display and said she preferred it from there. I was furious for days. It was quite good from there.”

He shows you a clip recorded earlier, before Miriam entered. The cabinet is closed; a small camera, inside on its own, records a stripe of light reflected from the water into the mirror. The stripe appears to hang in depthless black. Simon walks past the exterior camera while the interior image continues.

“The first time, I thought it was something my eyes did. So I put the camera where my head had been. Same line. I left. Same line. Something continues without my attendance. You do see why that matters?”

Miriam says that a tide continues without his attendance too.

“Of course. But I hadn’t seen the separation so cleanly. A view with nobody in it. For a few minutes I stopped trying to put myself behind every event. Then I thought: perhaps this is what space is when we aren’t furnishing it.”

“You had furnished it with a water tray and a camera.”

“And I can subtract their defects. Different cameras, different trays. If the relations survive every change we can make, what reason have we to deny that those relations belong to the world itself?”

Miriam does not answer immediately. Downstairs Ada drops a chair, swears at it, and puts it upright.

“That is a stronger question than the card you wrote,” Miriam says. “The card says you have shown reality with the conditions of looking removed. A camera changes the conditions. It doesn’t remove every condition under which anything can be given to us.”

Simon opens a folded card. His phrase is indeed there: “An unconditioned sight.” Under it he has drawn the cabinet carefully enough to include the screws. “I want to know what would be sufficient,” he says. “Not merely to be told this isn’t.”

He will show the longer recording of the incident. You can also ask him directly about the cord or return later to repeat the optical result without anyone enclosed.

## recording / recording.base

Simon hands over his phone with the sound already turned up. “The other clip was for the picture. This one was meant to show we hadn’t cut anything out.”

The camera has been set on the spectators’ bench. The whole cabinet and the pier are visible. Miriam enters by the open mirror door, turns and tests the ring while Simon holds the door just off its latch. Then he shuts it. For several seconds the seam glows.

Simon crosses in front of the camera with a spool. He passes a black cord through the rear loops and around the cabinet, across the door. He pulls the knot tight. The seam darkens. He goes back to the camera. Its view never changes.

For a while you hear the wash of the pool filter. Ada comes through the far doorway carrying the saucepan. She disappears into the workshop. Then a woman’s voice says, “Let me out.”

Simon, close to the microphone, says, “One minute. Let it settle.”

“Let me out.” The second time is louder. A click is audible. The cloth at the door moves outward and strains along the black cord.

Ada comes from the workshop without the pan. “Stand back if you can,” she says. She unfolds the hook knife and cuts the cord where it crosses the door’s outer edge. The door opens sharply. Its frame strikes the pier. Glass falls. Miriam comes out behind the moving door, bent forward and holding one hand.

Simon stops the clip. He has a thumb on the screen, covering nobody you need to see. You ask him to play it once more from the first request. He does. Nothing in the sequence changes.

“I heard her,” he says. “I thought she meant she couldn’t get the sight. I answered the thing I expected.”

The clip shows a sequence and contains a request. It does not show Miriam’s face while she is inside, or explain her physical state. It supplies no view into Simon’s mind before he spoke. It does show what his answer was.

You can compare the visible binding with the inside latch without accepting any private account. The door’s attempted movement stops at the cord; after the cut it opens. An empty reconstruction is still available if you want to examine that relation more closely.

## shared / shared.base

You put the plan on the bench. Ada weighs down one corner with the closed knife. Simon starts to put his phone on another, remembers the glass underneath it and chooses a different place.

You state the sequence you can support: an additional cord bound the cabinet, Miriam asked to leave, Ada cut the cord, the released door struck the pier and broke the mirror. The inside latch could work throughout. Simon acknowledges tying the cord.

“For the seam,” he says.

“For the seam,” you repeat. “That is your explanation of why.”

Miriam wants her request quoted exactly. Ada wants the words “deliberately destroyed” corrected. Simon wants the account to distinguish the failed exit from the sight. Nobody is asking for the same favor.

“You said you’d check the swing,” Ada tells him.

“You wrote it.”

“I told you.”

“I don’t remember.”

“All right. I remember telling you. You remember reading it. The mirror still hit the pier.”

He begins saying that they can keep extending responsibility backward, to the unpadded pier, the lack of room, whoever designed a bath with columns in the wrong place. Ada says the column held up the roof very well.

Miriam intervenes before he replies. “You can ask what made every one of us possible. I want to ask it too. But we don’t need the complete history of the world to decide whether to put someone back in that cabinet tomorrow.”

“I know,” Simon says. “I’m trying to work out where an explanation is allowed to stop.”

“For doing what?” Ada asks.

He looks at the plan. “For opening it tomorrow.”

“It stops there.” She touches the cord. “Take the door off. You can carry on with the other question while I do that.”

Miriam asks her to wait until you have kept whatever measurements you need. Ada puts the screwdriver down. This costs her a little effort. She has begun to enjoy the prospect of removing the door.

Simon picks up the small camera. “Before you take it apart, will you look at what it actually did? From outside. You can see every part.” Miriam asks Ada whether they can make an open model on a bench. Ada looks at the time, then goes to find something that will hold water.

## optics / optics.base

Simon takes a smaller mirror from his tool case. Ada brings a baking tray from the workshop and fills it with pool water. They set an open model of the arrangement on a bench: light slit, shallow water, inclined mirror. It does not require a door. Miriam sits where she can see everyone’s hands.

You put the camera where a person’s eye would be. Its screen shows a fine bright line in a dark field. It resembles a far horizon until you try to say how far. You move to the other side of the bench. The camera continues recording it.

At your request Ada covers the slit with a piece of card. The stripe disappears. She removes the card; it returns. Simon touches the tray. The stripe breaks into moving fragments and gathers itself as the water settles. No part of this result requires the earlier volunteer to have lied about what she saw.

“So we’ve found conditions of this sight,” Miriam says. “Light through there. Water still enough. A surface in that position. That doesn’t show us the conditions under which any outer object can appear to us at all.”

The window is still bright enough for the comparison, though the line is weaker than it was in the early clip. Simon says this is a model of the reflected stripe, not a repeat of Miriam’s whole experience in darkness. You mark that limitation beside the result.

Simon asks her to explain the difference without referring to a philosopher.

“You move the camera to find whether the stripe persists. To do that you already have to distinguish one place from another, your movement from the stripe’s movement, what happened before from what happened after. An experiment can discover new relations within that field. It cannot just switch the entire field off and still give you an object to inspect.”

“But why say the field belongs to our way of receiving things? The world might really be spatial, independently of us. Our way of receiving it might succeed because it belongs to that world. You haven’t shown otherwise with a piece of card.”

“No,” she says. “The card hasn’t proved that. You haven’t proved the opposite by leaving the camera in the room. The disagreement is about what warrants going from the structure of our experience to the structure of things considered apart from it.”

Ada shifts her weight. “Can you both still see the line?” They can. “Good. I need this hand back.” She gives you the card.

Simon watches the stripe. “When you say ‘apart from it,’ I picture another room. This one, with everything we contributed taken away. Then I want to get in there. I know that picture is doing something it hasn’t earned.”

“I don’t think there is a second workshop behind this one,” Miriam says. “I think thought and extension express the same reality, and we are finite ways that reality exists. Understanding a thing’s dependence isn’t peeking at a hidden copy of it.”

“And you know that same reality how? Through this understanding whose conditions you’ve just made so strict?”

Miriam leans forward until the peas slip from her hand. She lets the bag fall onto her lap. “That is where I disagree with the limit I was describing. I think understanding can go further than arranging appearances. I think we can know something of how a particular thing follows from Nature. It isn’t a view from outside. But I can’t borrow the camera as proof of that either.”

You ask whether Nature is then another name for everything piled together.

“More than the pile,” she says. “What exists in its own right, and what finite things depend on for being and being understood. If I call it God, I don’t mean someone watching to see whether we behave. I mean a cause that isn’t outside what it causes. I know that needs argument. It needs more than saying everything is connected.”

Simon says this sounds like the complete explanation he was being warned not to claim. Miriam asks him to put the card down while she answers. “You’ll make the answer into tomorrow’s title before I’ve finished.” He puts it down.

“Suppose what is fundamental has to be understood through itself,” she says, “rather than borrowing what it is from something else. You can’t get two such things just by pointing to different arrangements of the same nature. These bodies differ in position, motion, what they can do. I think those are real differences within extension, not separate pieces of self-existing being. Thought is another expression of what exists in its own right. It isn’t a little substance arriving to drive the bodies.”

“Why must it be the same being?” Simon asks. “Two things could have something in common without becoming one thing. And why must anything satisfy your definition of independence?”

“Ordinary things can share properties. I’m talking about what constitutes an entire nature, not whether two people have brown eyes. If you’ve given them the same complete nature, you still owe an account of what divides them. Their positions are already relations within extension. I don’t think you can use those relations to make separate ultimate sources of extension.”

“And thought?”

“I think the same argument has to hold. I haven’t shown that merely by saying it.” She moves her hand away from the tray. “But that is the reason I look for understanding through what a thing is, and not only through the last thing that happened to it. When I understand a particular dependence, I don’t think I am holding a picture outside Nature and comparing the two. I think that act of understanding belongs to Nature too.”

Simon says the act can belong to the world and still misdescribe it. Miriam agrees. For a moment neither can use the agreement to advance. Then Simon asks whether she will write out the argument, including the point about thought. She says yes, if he keeps his question about the definition at the top.

“Another evening,” he says, without withdrawing the question.

Ada wants the card. You pass it back. She covers half the slit to see what that will do. Simon bends to look. The disagreement has left them an experiment to carry out, though the experiment will not settle it.

## cabinet / cabinet.variant2

The brass ring is where it was. You pull it from outside and hear the same click. The latch withdraws. Nothing about that first sound needs to be withdrawn from your account.

You now know how the cord could hold the door after the latch released. The working handle was real; the conclusion that a person could therefore leave was another matter. Beside the ring, Ada fixes the two cord ends to the plan with a clothes peg. She does not tie them around the cabinet again.

The mirror frame’s dent is still level with the fresh chip on the concrete pier. Someone has put the felt pad on a chair. It never reached the part that struck.

Miriam watches from the bench. “When you write about this, don’t say I couldn’t find the handle. I found it. I did the thing I had been told to do.”

Simon answers before you can. “Yes.” Then he asks to see the wording.

The stripe lies across the surviving glass. The changed account of the door has not removed it.

## public_account / public_account.base

Simon types while you read the sequence. He pauses at his own name, then leaves it in. He reads the message aloud before sending it to the same group.

“The cord I added prevented the door opening. Miriam asked to leave. Ada cut it to get her out. The door struck the pier and the mirror broke. My earlier description of the experiment being deliberately destroyed was misleading. Tomorrow’s enclosed trial is canceled.”

Ada asks him to replace “misleading” with “wrong.” He looks at you. You point to the sequence you have just established. He makes the change.

The phone gives a small sound. One person replies with a thumbs-up. Another asks whether they can come and help clear the glass. Simon turns the sound off. You cannot know how twelve people will tell the story tomorrow, but they now have a correction from the person who sent the accusation.

“I wish you’d let me say it first,” he tells you.

You say he has said it now. He does not thank you for that.

Ada begins unscrewing the hinge. Miriam steadies the door from its undamaged edge. Simon stands without a task until Ada asks him for a box deep enough for the long fragments. He goes to find one.

The stripe is still on the open model. Nobody has offered it as a consolation for the public correction. It remains something Simon knows how to make, and something the others can examine.

## continuation / continuation.variant1

The door lies flat on two benches, wrapped in old towels. Simon has written the size of each recoverable piece on the wrapping. He asks permission to keep them until you agree what happens to the mirror. You give permission to store them, which is not an agreement about payment.

Simon has crossed out “An unconditioned sight” on the display card. Below it he has written: “A light effect that can be recorded without a viewer in the cabinet.” “Not much of an invitation,” he says. Then he adds the hours at which the open model will be available.

He has set the open optical model beside the diving board. “Different light tomorrow,” he says. “It will be worth seeing which measurements still hold.”

Miriam says she will watch from the gallery. “And you can ask me the question I didn’t answer.”

“How you know your one Nature.”

“Yes. Not whether being kind makes it true. The other one.”

“I still think we might learn that the relations belong to the world, not only to us,” he says to you. “There are failures I could discover. That seems better than repeating what the first success felt like.”

Miriam says she thinks understanding particular things can participate in a deeper understanding of what they depend on. Simon says this is still too quick. She asks him to bring that objection tomorrow rather than turn it into the title of another display.

Ada comes back with the mirror’s empty hooks. She has removed them from the door so nobody catches a sleeve. “You can use the room,” she tells him. “You can’t put anyone in a box. And if you need me on a Thursday, ask before six.”

Simon looks for a blank page in the folder. There is none. Miriam turns one of his rejected display cards over and puts it on the bench. He writes the question on the back, leaving enough space below for more than a reply.

There is food in the workshop. The potatoes have gone cold. Ada has found bread and something in a jar she is willing to identify only as “last year’s.” You can stay, or take your copy of the account and leave.

## supper / supper.base

Ada gives you the bread knife and a plate with a chip too small to warrant a warning. You notice it anyway. She tells you the pickle is marrow. Simon says he thought marrow was a crime committed by people with gardens. He takes some.

Miriam cannot cut her potato. She holds it with a fork while Ada cuts, an arrangement that requires them to move closer together. When Ada asks whether Thursday is really possible, Miriam opens her calendar and looks.

Simon begins describing what a second camera might establish. He stops when he realizes Miriam is still looking for Thursday. You eat for a while. Ada tries the pickle again and decides it could have used more salt.

Outside, somebody has taken in the white overalls. The window is an ordinary dark rectangle with the room in it. If you change your place, the reflected people move. Simon notices and lets it happen.

Before you leave, you look once more at the wrapping around the mirror. Your name is written on the largest piece. You correct one letter. The fragments remain in the bath by agreement, and the question of payment remains for a conversation you have not yet had.

When you put the pen down, it rolls into a fold in the towel. Ada takes it out before the next piece of glass is wrapped. She puts it beside your plate. There is still a slice of bread left.
